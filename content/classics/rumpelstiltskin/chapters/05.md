# Two Days of Guessing

When the evening came, the little man arrived. The queen was waiting for him. She sat on her chair with the baby in her arms, and the paper with all the names was on the table. She had a plan. She would try every name that she knew.

"Good evening, Your Majesty," said the little man. He stood in front of her with his hands behind his back. "Is it time? Do you know my name?"

"Let me think," said the queen. She looked at her paper. She began to read. "Is your name Caspar?"

"No," said the little man.

"Is it Melchior?"

"No."

"Is it Balthazar?"

"No."

She went down the list. She tried all the names that she had written. She said every name that she could think of. She said, "Henry," and "John," and "William," and "Peter." She said the names of kings, and the names of saints, and the names of heroes. But to each one, the little man shook his head, and he said, "That is not my name."

At last, she came to the end of her list. The little man smiled. He bowed to her, and he said, "I will come again tomorrow evening," and he went out.

The queen did not sleep that night. She stayed with the baby, and she looked at his sleeping face. She said to herself, "He must have a strange name. Not a name that anyone knows. I must find it."

In the morning, the messenger came back. He was tired and dusty, but his face was bright.

"Your Majesty," he said. "I have traveled far. I asked in every village. I have collected many strange names. Shall I read them?"

"Please," said the queen.

The messenger took out a piece of paper. He cleared his throat. "In the first village, there was a man called Ribs-of-Beef. In the second, a woman called Sheep-shanks. In the third, a boy called Laceleg. Then there was Crooked-nose, and Stick-in-the-mud, and Ten-toes."

The queen listened. Some of the names were funny. Some were very strange. She wrote them down. "Thank you," she said. "That is good. You have done well. Go and rest."

That evening, the little man came again. He looked at the queen with his bright eyes.

"Well, Your Majesty?" he said. "Do you know my name?"

"Perhaps," said the queen. She took a deep breath. "Is your name Ribs-of-Beef?"

"No," said the little man.

"Is it Sheep-shanks?"

"No."

"Is it Laceleg?"

"No, no, no," said the little man. He began to laugh. "That is not my name."

She tried them all. She tried Crooked-nose, and Stick-in-the-mud, and Ten-toes. She tried every strange name that the messenger had found. But to each one, the little man said, "No." His eyes were shining, and he rubbed his hands together.

"One more day," he said. "Tomorrow, the baby is mine." He bowed, and he went out of the room.

The queen put her face in her hands. She was very afraid. There was only one day left. She did not know what to do.

The next morning, she called the messenger again. He came in, and his face was serious.

"Your Majesty," he said. "I did not find any new names. I rode all day yesterday. I asked every person that I met. But there was nothing."

"Oh, no," said the queen. Her eyes filled with tears. "What will I do?"

"But," said the messenger slowly, "I saw something strange. Something that may help."

The queen looked up. "What was it?"

"I was riding through a high forest. It was far from here, near the edge of the mountains. The trees were thick, and the path was narrow. I had lost my way. I got down from my horse, and I walked a little. And then I saw a small house on a hill. It was a strange little house. In front of the house, there was a fire. A little man was jumping around the fire. He was hopping on one leg. And he was singing."

"A little man?" said the queen. She stood up. "What did he look like?"

"He was very small," said the messenger. "He had a long beard and a red hat. And he sang so loudly that I could hear every word."
