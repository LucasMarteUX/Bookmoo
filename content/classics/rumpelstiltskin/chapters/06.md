# The Song by the Fire

"What did he sing?" said the queen. Her heart was beating very fast. "Tell me, tell me every word."

The messenger closed his eyes to remember. Then, in a low voice, he sang it:

"Today I bake, tomorrow I brew,
The next day, the queen's child comes to me!
Oh, how glad I am that no one knows
That Rumpelstiltskin is my name!"

The queen stood very still. She said the name slowly, to herself. "Rumpelstiltskin." It was a long, strange name, and she had never heard it before.

"Say it again," she said.

"Rumpelstiltskin," said the messenger.

The queen laughed and cried at the same time. She took the messenger's hands. "You have saved my child," she said. "You have saved my life. Oh, thank you! I will give you a hundred pieces of gold."

"I did only what you asked, Your Majesty," said the messenger. He was smiling. "I am glad that I found the way."

All that day, the queen was happy and nervous. She played with the baby. She sang to him. She walked up and down in her room. She said the name again and again, so that she would not forget it. "Rumpelstiltskin, Rumpelstiltskin." She was afraid that when the moment came, she might say it wrongly.

At last, the evening came. The candles were lit, and the shadows grew long. The queen sat on her chair, with the baby in her arms. She tried to look calm. She even put on her finest dress.

The door opened, and the little man came in.

He was in a very good mood. He walked across the room with quick steps. His red hat was on one side, and his eyes shone. He rubbed his hands together.

"Well, Your Majesty!" he said. "It is the third day. The time is over. Do you know my name?"

The queen looked at him. She pretended to think. "Let me see," she said. "Is your name Conrad?"

"No," said the little man. He smiled.

"Is it Harry?"

"No."

She waited for a moment. Then she said, slowly, in a very clear voice: "Is your name perhaps... Rumpelstiltskin?"

The little man stood still. His smile vanished. His face went from red to white, and then back to red again. His eyes opened very wide. He stared at her.

"Who told you?" he cried. "Who told you that? The devil told you that! The devil!"

"It does not matter who told me," said the queen. She stood up, with the baby in her arms. She was not afraid any more. "You have lost, little man. I know your name. Now you must go."

The little man began to shake. He stamped his right foot on the floor. He stamped it so hard that it went into the stone, all the way up to his waist. He pulled and pulled, but his foot was stuck.

"The devil told you!" he screamed. He was so angry that his whole body shook. His face became purple. "The devil! The devil!"

In his rage, he took hold of his left foot with both hands. He pulled it with all his strength. He pulled and pulled. And then, with a loud tearing sound, he pulled himself in two.

The room was silent. The queen stood at the window with the baby in her arms. She did not look. She only held her child, and she felt his warm little breath against her neck. He was fast asleep.

Slowly, she let out a long breath.

"It is over," she whispered. "It is really over."

Nobody ever saw the little man again. The king never knew what had happened. The queen told him that a strange visitor had come to see her. He had gone away. She did not talk about the gold, or about the three nights. She did not want to remember them.

The baby grew up to be a strong, happy child. The queen watched him play in the garden, and she thanked God every day for her good messenger. She gave him a house of his own, and a bag of gold, as she had promised.

As for the miller, he stopped talking about his daughter. He learned that a man must be careful with his words. He sat in his little mill by the river and ground his wheat. And when someone asked him, "Is it true that your daughter can spin straw into gold?" he only shook his head and smiled.

"No," he said. "She can do something much better. She can keep her head when she is afraid."
