# The Little Man and the First Night

The miller's daughter sat down on the cold floor. She looked at the straw. She looked at the spinning wheel. She did not know how to turn straw into gold. Nobody did. She was alone in the room, and the king had locked the door. She was afraid, and she began to cry.

She cried for a long time. The sun went down behind the window, and the room became dark. A little light from the moon fell on the heaps of straw. She put her hands over her face. "I will die," she said. "I will die tomorrow, because of my father's words."

Suddenly, she heard a sound. It was a small click, and the door opened. She looked up.

A little man stood in the doorway. He was very small, no taller than a child of seven. He had a long grey beard and a pointed red hat. His eyes were bright and clever, and his nose was sharp. He walked into the room as if it were his own, and he closed the door behind him.

"Good evening, miller's daughter," he said. His voice was high and thin. "Why are you crying so bitterly?"

"Oh!" said the girl. She jumped to her feet. "How did you come in? The door was locked."

"I have my ways," said the little man. "But tell me. What is the matter?"

"I must spin this straw into gold," she said, and she pointed at the heaps. "And I do not know how. If I do not do it by morning, the king will kill me."

"Ah," said the little man. He looked at the straw, and he scratched his beard. "That is a hard job. What will you give me if I do it for you?"

The girl thought. She had nothing. She had no money, and no jewels. She only had her plain dress and her small shoes. At last, she touched her neck. There was a thin chain there, with a little glass bead. It was a present from her mother.

"I will give you my necklace," she said.

The little man looked at it. He took it between his fingers. He nodded his head. "It will do," he said.

He sat down at the spinning wheel. He put the first handful of straw on it. Whirr, whirr, whirr! The wheel went round three times, and the straw turned into a thick golden thread. It fell on the floor, bright and shining. The little man put on a second handful. Whirr, whirr, whirr! Another golden thread. He worked very fast. He did not stop. Whirr, whirr, whirr! Again and again, the wheel turned. The golden threads grew into a great pile. The straw grew smaller and smaller.

The miller's daughter stood in a corner, and she watched. She did not dare to speak. She felt as if she were in a dream.

When the morning came, the sun shone through the window. All the straw was gone. In its place, there was a mountain of gold. It was thread after thread of shining gold, as high as her waist. The little man stood up. He put the necklace in his pocket. He bowed to her, and he went out of the door without a word.

A moment later, she heard the key in the lock. The king came in. He looked at the room. His eyes opened wide. He could hardly believe what he saw.

"Gold!" he said. "So much gold!" He walked around it. He touched it, and he laughed out loud. He was happy. But he was also greedy. The more he had, the more he wanted.

He took the miller's daughter by the hand. "You have done well," he said. "I am pleased. But now I want more. Tonight, you will go to a bigger room. There is more straw there. If you spin that into gold, too, I will let you live."

She could not say no. He took her to a room twice as big as the first one. It was full of straw, from the floor to the ceiling. In the middle stood a spinning wheel. The king went out and turned the key. The girl was alone again.

She sat on the floor and cried. She did not know what to do.

Then the door opened, and the little man came in again.
