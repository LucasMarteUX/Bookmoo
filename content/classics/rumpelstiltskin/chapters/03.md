# The Second and Third Nights

"Good evening again," said the little man. "You are crying again. Is it the straw?"

"Oh, yes," said the miller's daughter. She wiped her eyes. "The king wants more gold. This room is twice as big as the last one. I cannot do it. I do not know how you did it. Please help me once more."

"What will you give me this time?" said the little man.

The girl looked at her hands. She had nothing left. Then she saw the ring on her finger. It was a small gold ring that she wore every day. It had belonged to her grandmother, and she loved it very much. She took a deep breath.

"I will give you my ring," she said.

The little man took it. He held it up to the light, and he smiled. "It is a pretty ring," he said. "I accept it."

He sat down at the wheel. Whirr, whirr, whirr! The straw turned into gold, and the gold fell on the floor. He worked all night, and he did not rest. The miller's daughter sat and watched. She was tired, but she did not sleep. At dawn, the room was full of shining gold. There was twice as much as before.

The little man put on the ring. He walked out of the room without a word. And the girl heard the king's footsteps on the stairs.

The king came in. When he saw all the gold, his face became pink with joy. He ran from one heap to another. He picked up handfuls of golden thread, and he let them run through his fingers. He laughed like a child.

"This is wonderful," he said. "You are a clever girl. But you know, I am still not satisfied. A king can never have too much gold. Tonight, you will go to the biggest room in the castle. It is full of straw. If you spin it all into gold by morning, I will make you my wife. You will be queen of this country. It is not a bad idea, is it? A girl who can make gold, for a wife?"

The miller's daughter stared at him. She did not want to marry a man who thought only of gold. But she knew that she had no choice. If she said no, he might kill her.

"Yes, Your Majesty," she said quietly.

He took her to the biggest room in the castle. It was so big that you could hardly see the far wall. Everywhere, there was straw. It was as high as a house. It was enough to fill a hundred barns. In the middle, a lonely little wheel stood.

When the king had gone, the miller's daughter sat down on the floor. She did not cry this time. She was too tired and too afraid. She only sat and looked at the wall.

At midnight, the little man came in.

"Well, miller's daughter," he said. "Here is the biggest room. What will you give me this time?"

"I have nothing left," said the girl. She opened her hands. "My necklace is gone, and my ring is gone. I have only my dress and my shoes. If you want them, you may have them."

The little man shook his head. He walked around the room, and he touched the straw. He looked at her with his bright, clever eyes. Then he said, in a very quiet voice:

"Promise me something else. Promise me that when you are queen, you will give me your first child."

The miller's daughter did not answer at once. She thought of the straw. She thought of the king and his gold. She did not know if she would ever be queen. She did not know if she would ever have a child. It seemed so far away.

"Who knows what will happen?" she thought. "Perhaps it will never come to that. I must save my life now."

"I promise," she said.

The little man smiled. He rubbed his hands together. He sat down at the wheel, and he began to work. Whirr, whirr, whirr! The straw became gold. It went on and on, all night. By morning, the great room was full of gold from the floor to the ceiling. It shone so brightly that it hurt the eyes.

The little man stood up. He bowed to the girl. He did not say goodbye.

In the morning, the king came. He saw the gold, and he cried out with joy. He called all his servants to see it. He called his ministers and his soldiers. Then he turned to the miller's daughter. He took her hand.

"You have kept your promise," he said. "And I keep mine. You will be my wife."

So the miller's daughter became the queen. There was a big wedding in the castle. There was music and dancing, and the people shouted, "Long live the queen!" The miller sat at the table, in a new coat. He was very proud. He told everyone that he had always known that his daughter was special. But in the evenings, when the queen was alone, she sat at her window and looked out at the dark garden. She tried not to think of the little man.
