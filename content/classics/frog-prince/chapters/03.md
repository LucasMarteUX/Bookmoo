# Tap, Tap, Plash, Plash

The youngest princess jumped up. She ran to the door and opened it. In front of her, on the cold marble floor, sat the frog.

She screamed. She slammed the door shut, and she ran back to the table. She sat down quickly, and her heart was beating very fast. Her face was white.

The king looked at her. He was a wise man, and he saw that something had frightened his daughter.

"My child," he said, "why are you afraid? Is there a giant at the door? Does he want to carry you away?"

"Oh, no," said the princess. "It is not a giant. It is a nasty frog."

"A frog?" said the king. He smiled a little.

"Yes, Father," said the princess. "Yesterday, I was playing in the forest, by the well. I threw my golden ball in the air, and it fell into the water. I cried, and then this frog came, and he said that he would bring it back. But he said that I must promise to let him be my friend. I did not think that he could ever come to the castle. I thought that he must stay in the well. But now he is here, and he wants to come in!"

Outside, there was a second knock. The little voice cried again:

"Youngest princess, my love,
Open the door for me!
Do you not remember what you said
At the cool well yesterday?
Youngest daughter of the king,
Open the door for me!"

"Oh, Father!" said the princess. She put her hands on her cheeks. "I cannot stand it. He is so wet, and so slimy. Please do not make me let him in."

The king was silent for a moment. He looked at his youngest daughter. She was a good girl, but she was often thoughtless. Then he said, in a serious voice, "What you have promised, you must do. That is the rule of this house. The frog brought back your ball, and you gave your word. Go and let him in."

"But Father—"

"Go," said the king.

The princess stood up slowly. She went to the door, and she opened it. The frog hopped into the room. Tap, tap, plash, plash. He hopped across the floor, all the way to the table, and he stopped at the princess's chair. The sisters looked at him, and they moved their chairs away. One of them made a face.

"Lift me up," said the frog, "and let me sit by you."

The princess did not move. She looked at the king.

"Lift him up," said the king.

She took a handkerchief out of her sleeve. She wrapped the frog in it, and she put him on the chair beside her. He sat very still, with his big eyes turned toward her.

"Now push your plate a little nearer," said the frog, "so that we can eat together."

The princess did it. The frog began to eat from her plate. He ate with great pleasure. But the princess could hardly swallow a bite. Her throat was tight. All her sisters stared at her. The servants looked away, and some of them smiled behind their hands.

At last, when the frog had eaten all that he could, he said, "I am quite full. But I am also tired. Please carry me upstairs to your little room, and put me in your pretty silk bed. We will sleep there together."

The princess began to cry. She was afraid of the frog's cold, wet body. She did not even like to touch him with her finger. And now he wanted to sleep in her clean, pretty bed!

But the king looked at her angrily. "You must not despise someone who helped you when you were in trouble," he said.

So she took the frog between two fingers. She carried him upstairs, and she put him in a corner of her room, on the floor.

"No, no," said the frog. "I am tired. I want to sleep in your bed. Lift me up. If you do not, I will tell your father."

The princess was very angry now. She took him up, and she threw him on the bed. Not in the middle, only on the edge, as far from her pillow as she could. She said, "There. Now, are you happy?"

But she did not sleep in that bed. She lay down on a chair in the corner and cried until she fell asleep.
