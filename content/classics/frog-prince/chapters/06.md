# Faithful Henry and the Iron Bands

Faithful Henry was the young prince's servant. He was an old man now, with grey hair and a kind face. When his master was turned into a frog, Henry was very sad. He loved the prince so much that he thought his heart would break. So he did a strange thing. He had three strong iron bands put around his chest, over his heart, so that it could not break from grief.

For all those years, Henry had worn the bands. He had waited with the coach and the eight white horses, and he had hoped that one day he would see his master again. Now the day had come.

The prince ran out of the castle. "Henry!" he cried. "Dear Henry! I am free!"

Henry jumped down from the coach. He was crying and laughing. He fell on his knees in front of the prince. "My master," he said. "My dear master. I have waited so long. I could not bear to think of you in the cold, wet well."

"Get up, my friend," said the prince. He lifted the old man to his feet and hugged him. "It is over. And look. I have found a wife."

Henry turned to the princess. He took off his hat and bowed very low. "My lady," he said. "I thank you from my heart. I do not know how to say it. You have given me back my master."

The princess smiled, and she gave the old man her hand. "I think that I owe him more than he owes me," she said.

The prince and the princess said goodbye to the king and the sisters. They hugged them, and they promised to visit. Then the prince helped his bride into the coach. He sat next to her, and Henry climbed onto his seat behind. He took the reins in his hands.

"To the kingdom!" said the prince.

The eight white horses started to move. The coach rolled out of the courtyard, and through the gate, and along the road. They went through the green fields, and past the forest, and the old lime tree and the cool well. The princess looked out of the window. She saw the place where she had lost her ball. She smiled and shook her head.

They had not gone far when the prince heard a strange sound behind them.

It was a loud crack, like the sound of a breaking branch.

"What was that?" he said. "Henry, has something broken in the coach?"

"No, master," called Henry, from the box. "It is not the coach. It is a band from my heart. I put it there when you were in the well, so that my heart would not break with sadness. Now my heart is so full of joy that the band has broken."

They drove on. After a while, there was a second crack, louder than the first.

"Henry?" said the prince.

"It is only another band, master," said Henry. His voice was happy. "It is nothing at all."

A little farther on, they heard a third crack. They were almost at the border of the prince's kingdom. The mountains were green, and the sky was blue, and a soft wind blew across the fields.

"That is the last one," said Henry. He laughed out loud. "My heart is free. Oh, master, I did not know that I could be so glad."

The prince laughed, too. He took the princess's hand, and he held it tight. The coach rolled on, and the horses ran faster. They came at last to the prince's country, and they saw the high castle in the green hills.

The people came out of their houses and cheered. The bells rang. The old king and queen ran out of the gate. They had been waiting for their son for such a long time. They hugged him and cried, and they welcomed the princess as their own daughter.

There was a great wedding. There was music in the streets, and a feast in the castle for three days. And the prince and the princess lived happily in the high castle for many, many years. On warm evenings, they sat together in the garden. Sometimes the princess took out her golden ball, and she threw it up in the air and caught it again. And sometimes, from the pond in the garden, a little green frog looked up at her, and she smiled.
