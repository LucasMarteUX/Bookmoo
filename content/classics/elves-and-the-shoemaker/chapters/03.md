# Two Little Men at Midnight

"What is your idea?" asked the shoemaker.

"It is almost Christmas," said his wife. "For many weeks, someone has been working for us at night. Someone has made us rich. We do not know who it is. I think that we should find out. Tonight, we will not go to bed. We will stay up and watch. We will hide in the corner of the room, behind the clothes that hang on the wall. Then we will see who comes."

The shoemaker thought about it. He was a little afraid. But he was also very curious. "It is a good idea," he said. "I would like to thank the helper in person."

So they did not go to bed. They lit a small candle, and they left it burning on the bench. Then they went to the corner of the room, where the old coats and cloaks hung on pegs. They stepped behind them and stood quite still. Through a space between two coats, they could see the whole shop.

The hours went slowly. The clock ticked on the wall. The wind sighed in the chimney. The candle burned lower. The shoemaker's legs began to hurt, and his wife yawned. But they did not move.

At last, the clock struck twelve. Its slow, deep sound filled the room.

At the very same moment, there was a sound at the door. It was a tiny click, no louder than a mouse. The door opened a little way. Two small shapes slipped in. They ran across the floor on tiptoe, as fast as two kittens, and they jumped up on the bench.

They were two little men. They were no bigger than a child's doll. They had round, rosy faces, and big bright eyes. They had curly hair, and pointed ears. Their hands were small and quick. And they had no clothes on at all.

The shoemaker and his wife held their breath.

The two little men did not look around. They did not say a word. They went straight to the leather pieces on the bench. Each of them took a needle and thread. They sat down side by side on the wood, with their little legs hanging over the edge. They began to stitch.

Oh, how fast they worked! Their fingers flew. The needles went in and out, in and out. They pulled the thread tight, and they tapped the soles with tiny hammers. They did not stop. They did not even look at each other. They did their work with great skill, as if they had done it a thousand times. One of them whistled very softly. The other smiled to himself.

The shoemaker watched with wide eyes. He had never seen such fast and clever work. In a short time, one pair of shoes was finished. The first little man set it aside. They began on the next pair. Pair after pair, they finished the work. The shoes stood in a neat row on the bench, shining in the candlelight.

When the last shoe was done, the little men jumped down from the bench. They looked at their work for a moment, and they nodded. Then they ran to the door, as lightly as they had come. In a second, they were gone.

The shop was quiet again. The candle was nearly out.

The shoemaker and his wife stepped out from behind the coats. For a long time, they did not speak. They looked at the shoes. Then they looked at each other.

"Little men," said the shoemaker at last. His voice shook. "Two little men. They had no clothes on. They must be so cold in the winter."

"They have made us rich," said his wife. "And they have never asked for anything. I have been thinking, husband. I cannot bear it. They run about in the snow with nothing on their little bodies. I would like to do something for them. I will make each of them a shirt, and a coat, and trousers, and a pair of warm stockings. I will make them as small as I can."

"That is a good thought," said the shoemaker. "And I will make each of them a pair of little shoes. They will fit them perfectly. I have been making shoes for a long time, but I never made any as small as those."

So the next day, they began. The wife took out her best cloth, and she cut it very small. She sewed the shirts, and the little coats, and the tiny trousers. She even knitted two pairs of stockings, as small as thimbles. The shoemaker took the finest leather, and he made two pairs of little shoes. They were so tiny that they could stand on the palm of his hand. He was very careful. He used all his skill.

When all was ready, they looked at the clothes, laid out on the table. They were so small and pretty that they both smiled.

"I hope that they like them," said the wife.

"I think that they will," said the shoemaker.
