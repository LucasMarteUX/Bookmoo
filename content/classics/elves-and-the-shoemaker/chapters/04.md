# The Christmas Gift

It was Christmas Eve. The snow was falling softly, and the church bells were ringing across the town. In the little house with the green door, the fire burned warm and bright. The shoemaker and his wife had eaten a good supper. Now they stood in the shop.

On the bench, in place of the leather, they put the little presents. There were two small shirts, white as snow. There were two tiny coats of green cloth with brass buttons. There were two pairs of trousers, two pairs of stockings, and two pairs of pointed caps. And in front of them, side by side, stood the two pairs of tiny leather shoes. They looked like toys.

"It is all ready," said the wife. She straightened one of the little coats. She touched the soft cloth with her finger. "I hope that they will come."

"They always come," said the shoemaker. "They have never missed a night."

He blew out the big lamp, but he left a small candle burning on the shelf. Then they went to the corner and stepped behind the old coats on the wall. They stood very still, and they waited.

The night went by slowly. The snow hushed the street, and everything was quiet. At last, when the clock began to strike twelve, they heard the little click at the door.

The door opened. The two little men ran in on their bare feet. They jumped on the bench, with their needles ready. They were going to begin their work. But when they looked down, they stopped.

There was no leather on the bench. In its place, there was a pile of small clothes.

The little men looked at each other. They looked at the clothes. For a moment, they did not move. They did not understand. Then one of them touched a little green coat, very gently, with the tip of his finger. The other picked up a pair of shoes. He turned them over and over in his hands.

"Look," he said, in a tiny, high voice. "They are for us!"

"They are real clothes," said the other. "Clothes for the elves!"

A big smile came on their faces. They looked at the little shirts, and the coats, and the trousers. They jumped for joy. They put on the shirts first, and then the trousers and the stockings. They pulled on the coats and fastened the buttons. They put the pointed caps on their curly heads. Last of all, they sat down on the bench, and they put on the shoes. They fit perfectly.

Then the elves stood up. They looked at each other from head to foot. They were two handsome little gentlemen in new green coats. They turned around, and they bowed. They laughed with joy.

"Now we are boys so fine to see," they sang, in their high, sweet voices. "Why should we cobble any more? Why should we cobble for you and me?"

They took each other by the hand. They began to dance. They jumped over the bench. They hopped over the chairs. They danced round the shop, in and out of the shadows, across the floor and up on the shelves, until the candle shook. The shoemaker and his wife watched from behind the coats, and they smiled. Tears of joy ran down the wife's face.

At last, the little men came to the door. They stopped for a moment. They looked back at the bench, and they bowed once more, very low. Then they danced out into the snow. The door closed softly behind them.

The shop was quiet.

The shoemaker and his wife came out from their hiding place. They looked at the empty bench, and then at each other.

"They are gone," said the wife.

"They are," said the shoemaker. "And I think that they will not come back."

"I am glad that we made the clothes," said the wife.

"So am I," said the shoemaker. And he meant it.

The little men did not come back. But the shoemaker never became poor again. His luck stayed with him, and his work went well. He had learned to make shoes as fine as the elves' shoes, and people came from all over the country to buy them. He had so many customers that he took on two helpers, and soon he had a big shop on the main street. His wife wore a new shawl and a silver ring. They had enough to eat, and a fire in every room, and some money to give to the poor.

On every Christmas Eve, the shoemaker and his wife put a little bowl of milk and a small piece of cake on the bench. In the morning, the bowl was always empty. And they would smile, and say a quiet thank you to the two little friends who had helped them so long ago.
