# The House of Bricks

The third little pig was not in a hurry. He walked along his road for a long time, and he looked at the trees and the flowers. He thought about his brothers. "I hope that they are safe," he said. "But a house of straw or sticks is not for me. I want something that will last."

At the end of the road, he met a man with a big cart. The cart was full of red bricks. They were heavy and hard, and they were piled high.

"Good afternoon, sir," said the third little pig. "Please, give me those bricks. I want to build a house."

"Bricks?" said the man. "That is a lot of work, little pig. They are very heavy. Are you sure?"

"I am sure," said the little pig. "I will work hard. A strong house is worth a lot of work."

The man looked at him and smiled. "You are a clever little pig," he said. "Here. Take them."

The little pig thanked him. He took the bricks to a sunny hill at the edge of the wood. He began to build. It was hard work. The bricks were heavy, and he got hot and dirty. He had to carry them one by one. He mixed mud and sand to make a strong paste, and he put a little between each brick. He built the walls straight and thick. It took him many days.

His hands were sore, and his back hurt. But he did not stop. Every morning, he got up early and worked until it was dark. Every evening, he sat on the step and looked at what he had done. And slowly, the house grew.

At last, it was finished. It was a neat red house with a heavy wooden door, two windows with shutters, and a tall brick chimney. The walls were as thick as his arm. In the kitchen, there was a big fireplace and a big iron pot. In the garden, there was a patch of earth for vegetables.

"There," said the third little pig. He wiped his forehead. "That is a good house. Now I can sleep safe at night."

A few days later, the wolf came.

He came through the trees, slowly and quietly. He had finished with the straw house and the stick house. He was hungry again. And when he saw the red house on the hill, he licked his lips.

He walked up to the front door, and he knocked.

Knock, knock, knock.

"Who is there?" said the third little pig. He was in the kitchen, stirring a pot of soup.

"Little pig, little pig," said the wolf, in a sugar-sweet voice, "let me come in."

The little pig looked at the door. He knew that voice. He had heard about it from his mother. He was not afraid. He walked to the door, and said in a loud, clear voice:

"No, no, not by the hair of my chinny chin chin!"

"Then I will huff, and I will puff, and I will blow your house in!" said the wolf.

He took a deep breath, and he blew. He huffed. He puffed. He huffed and he puffed. He puffed and he huffed. His face became red, and his eyes became big. He blew until his sides hurt.

But the house did not move. Not one brick shook. Not one window rattled. The red walls stood as firm as a rock.

The wolf was very angry. He tried again. He took the deepest breath that he could, and he blew until he was dizzy. He blew so hard that he fell over backwards. But the house stayed where it was.

"I cannot blow it down," he said at last, as he lay on the ground. "This is a strong house."

He sat up and thought. He was a clever wolf, and he did not give up so easily. He brushed the dust from his fur, and he put on a friendly smile. He walked up to the window and spoke in a different voice.

"Little pig," he said, "you are a very clever pig. Let us be friends. I know where there is a very nice field of turnips."

"Where?" said the little pig, through the window.
