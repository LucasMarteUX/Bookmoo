# The Clever Little Pig and the Wolf

"Oh, in Mr. Smith's home field," said the wolf. "It is full of the best turnips. If you will be ready tomorrow morning, I will come for you, and we will go together to get some for dinner."

"That is a good idea," said the little pig. "What time will you come?"

"At six o'clock," said the wolf.

"Very well," said the little pig. "I will be ready."

But the little pig was not a fool. As soon as the wolf went away, he said to himself, "He wants to eat me in the field. I will not be there at six. I will go earlier."

The next morning, the little pig got up at five o'clock. He took a basket, and he ran to Mr. Smith's field. He pulled up a big pile of turnips, and he took them home to his kitchen. He put them in the pot and lit the fire. At six o'clock, the wolf arrived at the door.

"Little pig," said the wolf. "Are you ready?"

"Ready?" said the little pig. "I have already been, and I have come back. I have a nice potful of turnips for my dinner."

The wolf felt very angry. He had come to the field and found nothing. But he did not show it. He said to himself, "I will trick him another way."

"Little pig," he said, "I know where there is a nice apple tree."

"Where?" said the little pig.

"Down at Merry Garden," said the wolf. "I will come for you at five o'clock tomorrow, and we will go together to pick some apples. Do not trick me this time."

"Very well," said the little pig. "I will be ready."

But the next morning, the little pig got up at four o'clock. He took his basket, and he went off to Merry Garden. It was farther than he thought. He had to climb the tree, and it was a big one, and the apples were high up. He was in the top of the tree, filling his basket, when he heard a sound.

He looked down. The wolf was coming along the path.

The little pig's heart jumped. He could not climb down fast enough. He did not know what to do. The wolf came to the foot of the tree and looked up with a big smile.

"Little pig," he said. "You are here before me! Are the apples good?"

"Yes, they are very good," said the little pig, as calmly as he could. "Wait. I will throw one down to you."

He picked a big red apple. He threw it. He threw it as far as he could, much farther than the wolf was standing. It fell in the long grass, far away. While the wolf ran to pick it up, the little pig jumped down from the tree and ran home as fast as his legs could carry him.

The next day, the wolf came to the door again. He had a plan.

"Little pig," he said, "there is a fair in the town this afternoon. Will you go with me?"

"Oh, yes," said the little pig. "I love fairs. What time shall we go?"

"At three," said the wolf.

The little pig went off before the time, as usual. He walked to the fair, and he looked at all the stalls. There were so many things to see: hats and ribbons, sweets and cakes, pots and pans. At one stall, he found a fine wooden butter churn. He bought it with his last coin, and he put it on his back.

But as he started home, he saw the wolf on the road, coming toward him.

There was no place to hide. There was no tree and no house. The little pig looked at the churn. It was a big wooden barrel, tall and round. He took the lid off. He climbed inside, and he pulled the lid down. He was so frightened that he began to shake, and the churn began to move. It rolled over, and it began to roll down the hill, faster and faster.

The wolf saw a great round thing coming at him down the hill. It shook and it bumped and it made a very loud noise. He had never seen such a thing. He screamed, and he turned, and he ran away as fast as he could. He did not even go to the fair.

That evening, he came to the little pig's house, and he knocked on the door.

"Little pig," he said, still out of breath, "I went to the fair, but I did not get there. On the way, I saw a terrible thing. It was a great round monster, and it rolled down the hill and nearly ran me over."

The little pig laughed. "Ha ha!" he said. "I frightened you, did I? It was not a monster. I had been to the fair, and I bought a butter churn. When I saw you, I got inside it, and I rolled down the hill."

The wolf was so angry that his whole face became red. His fur stood on end. He stamped his foot.

"I will eat you!" he cried. "I will come down your chimney!"

The little pig did not say anything. He went into the kitchen, and he put a big pot of water on the fire. He put more wood on it. He made the fire blaze, and the water began to boil. It boiled and it bubbled. Steam rose from the pot.

Then he heard a noise on the roof. Scratch, scratch. The wolf was climbing. He crept up the wall, and he stood on the tiles, and he looked down into the chimney. It was dark and wide. A little smoke came out of it.

"Here I come," whispered the wolf. "Ready or not."

He put his legs into the chimney, and he began to slide. Down, down, down, he went in the dark. He could see the light of the fire at the bottom, and he could smell the soup.

The little pig stood next to the pot. He waited. He held the lid in both hands. The wolf came down, nearer and nearer. At the very moment when the wolf's feet came out of the chimney, the little pig lifted the lid.

SPLASH!

The wolf fell into the boiling water. He gave one great cry. And the little pig put the lid back on the pot, quickly and firmly, and he held it down with all his strength.

Then it was quiet.

That evening, the little pig ate a good dinner. He sat at his table in the red house, and he ate with a big spoon. The fire burned warm and bright. He was tired, but he was happy.

"A strong house," he said, "and a clever head. They are the best things in the world."

He lived in his brick house for many years, in peace and comfort, and he was never afraid again.
