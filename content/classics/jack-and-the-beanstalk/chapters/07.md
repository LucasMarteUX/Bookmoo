# The Giant Falls

Jack ran. He ran out of the door and down the long straight road as fast as his legs would carry him. He held the golden harp tight against his chest. Behind him, the giant came after him. Thump! Thump! Thump! The whole land shook with his steps.

"Stop, thief!" shouted the giant. "Bring back my harp!"

Jack did not stop. He did not look back. He knew the road well now, and he knew where he was going. The giant was so big and so fast that he would soon catch him. But Jack was small and quick. He dodged, and he turned, and he jumped over stones. Once, the giant's big hand almost closed around him, and Jack felt the wind of it on his neck.

"Master! Master!" cried the harp. "He is escaping!"

"Be quiet!" said Jack. "Please be quiet!"

At last, he saw the edge of the white land and the green top of the beanstalk. He ran to it, and he threw himself on to the stalk. He began to climb down. His hands were sweating, and his heart was beating wildly. He climbed down faster than he had ever climbed in his life.

Above him, he heard a sound that made him look up. The giant stood at the edge of the white land. He looked down at the beanstalk. His face was purple with anger. Then he took hold of the stalk with his huge hands, and he put his foot on it, and he began to climb down after Jack.

The beanstalk shook. Its leaves rustled. It bent under the giant's weight.

"Oh no," whispered Jack. "He is coming."

He climbed down faster. His arms hurt, and his fingers were red. He went down through the white cloud and out of the other side. The earth came in sight, small and green. The giant was behind him, nearer and nearer. Jack could hear his heavy breath. He could feel the beanstalk shake with every step.

"Mother!" he shouted. "Mother! Mother!"

At last, his feet touched the ground. He fell on the grass and rolled over. He jumped up. He looked up at the beanstalk. The giant was halfway down. His legs and his big boots were coming through the clouds.

Jack's mother ran out of the house. She had heard him shout.

"Jack! What is it? What is the matter?"

"Mother, quick!" said Jack. "Bring me the hatchet! Bring me the axe! Quick, quick!"

She did not ask any questions. She ran to the shed, and she took the axe from the wall. She ran back as fast as she could, and she gave it to him. It was a good, sharp axe. Jack took it in both hands. He ran to the beanstalk.

He lifted the axe, and he hit the stalk with all his strength. Chop! The beanstalk shook. He hit again. Chop! Chop! Pieces of green wood flew in the air. Above, he heard the giant roar. The giant saw what Jack was doing. He tried to climb down faster. But it was too late.

Jack gave one last great blow. The beanstalk cracked. It tilted. It began to fall.

"Stand back, Mother!" shouted Jack.

The beanstalk fell slowly, with a long groan, like a great tree in a storm. The giant fell with it. He tried to hold on, but he could not. His huge body came down through the sky. He fell across the field behind the house, and the earth shook. There was a great noise, like a thunderclap. A cloud of dust rose up.

When the dust settled, the giant lay in the field. He did not move. He had fallen so hard that he made a great hole in the ground. The beanstalk lay in pieces around him. The leaves were already turning brown.

Jack and his mother stood together and looked. They did not say anything. Their hearts were beating very fast. The villagers came running from their houses, and they stood in a circle around the hole, and they looked at the giant. They were silent with wonder.

The giant was dead.

"It is over," said Jack's mother at last. She put her hand on her son's shoulder. "It is over, Jack. We are safe."

"Yes," said Jack. He was shaking. He looked at the golden harp, which he still held in his arm. It had stopped talking. It sat quietly, shining in the sun.

Then the harp began to sing. Its voice was soft and sweet. It sang of the green fields and the quiet rivers. It sang of peace and gladness, and it filled the whole garden with music. The villagers took off their hats. Some of them cried.

After that day, Jack and his mother lived in peace for the rest of their lives. They had the golden hen, which laid its egg every morning, and the harp, which sang to them every evening. They never wanted for money again. They gave much of it to the poor of the village, and they were loved by all their neighbors.

Jack changed, too. He did not lie under the trees anymore, and he did not say, "In a minute." He learned to farm and to read and to write, and he worked hard every day. When he was a man, he married a kind young woman from the village. They had many children, and he told them the story of the beanstalk by the fire in the evenings.

"Is it true, Father?" the children asked.

"Every word," said Jack, with a smile. "But you must never sell a cow for a handful of beans."

And they all lived happily for many, many years.
