# Fee-Fi-Fo-Fum

Jack had not finished half of his bread when the whole house began to shake.

Thump! Thump! Thump!

The cups on the shelf rattled. A pot fell from its hook. The floor trembled under Jack's feet. Somebody was coming, and it was somebody very, very big.

"Goodness gracious!" cried the big woman. Her face went white. "It is my husband! He is home! What shall I do? Quick, quick, come here! Jump in here!"

She picked up Jack in her big hand, and she pushed him into the great stone oven in the wall. She closed the door. Jack lay on the cold bricks, and he did not dare to breathe. Through a crack in the door, he could see part of the kitchen.

The kitchen door opened with a crash. In came the giant.

He was a giant in truth. He was so tall that he had to bend his head under the roof beam. His shoulders were as wide as a door. His hands were as big as shovels. He had a red face and a black beard, and little angry eyes. On his belt, he had three calves tied by the legs. He took them off, and he threw them on the table with a bang.

"Here, wife!" he said, in a voice like thunder. "Cook me two of these for my breakfast. I am as hungry as a wolf!"

He sat down in his great chair. It creaked. Then he stopped. He lifted his nose, and he sniffed. He sniffed again. A terrible smile came on his face.

"Ah," he said. "What is this I smell?

"Fee-fi-fo-fum,
I smell the blood of an Englishman.
Be he alive or be he dead,
I will grind his bones to make my bread!"

Jack nearly stopped breathing. His heart was beating so loudly that he was afraid the giant would hear it.

"Nonsense, dear," said the big woman. She turned from the fire with a wooden spoon in her hand. "You are dreaming. Or perhaps you smell the leftovers of the little boy that you ate for dinner yesterday. Do not worry about it. Go and wash your hands and your face. By the time you come back, your breakfast will be ready."

The giant grumbled. He stood up and went out of the room. His big feet shook the house with every step.

The woman hurried to the oven, and she opened the door a little. "Do not move," she whispered. "Wait until he is asleep. He always sleeps after breakfast. Then you can go."

"Thank you," whispered Jack.

The giant came back. He ate the two calves, one after the other, and he drank a barrel of beer. Then he leaned back in his chair and rubbed his belly. "Now, wife," he said. "Bring me my money."

The big woman went to a big chest. She took out two big bags, and she put them on the table. They were heavy, and they clinked. The giant opened one of the bags, and he poured out a river of gold coins. He began to count them. He counted them with his big thick fingers, one at a time. "One, two, three, four..." He counted, and he counted, and he counted. The pile of coins grew higher.

Slowly, the giant's head began to nod. His eyes closed. His mouth fell open. After a short time, he began to snore. It was a great loud snore, like the noise of a mill. The whole house shook with it. The cups rattled again, and the plates danced on their shelf.

"Now," whispered the big woman. She opened the oven. "Quickly, boy. Go."

Jack crept out of the oven on tiptoe. He did not make a sound. He looked at the giant. The giant's head lay on the table next to the gold. His mouth was open, and a long string of breath came out. Jack passed very near him. He could see the black hairs in his nose.

On the table, there was a bag of gold. It was a big, heavy leather bag. Jack took it in his arms. It was so heavy that he almost fell. But he held on, and he walked on tiptoe to the door, and out into the white land. Then he ran.

He ran down the long straight road as fast as he could, with the bag in his arms. He did not stop until he came to the edge of the world, and the great green stalk. He threw the bag over the edge. It fell down and down, and it landed in his mother's garden with a heavy thump. Then he swung himself on to the beanstalk, and he began to climb down.

He climbed and climbed. When he got to the bottom, he was so tired that he could hardly walk. But he ran into the house. His mother was in the kitchen, and she had been crying all night. When she saw him, she jumped up and took him in her arms.

"Jack! Jack! Where have you been? I was so worried! I looked everywhere! I looked in your bed, and you were not there!"

"Mother," said Jack. He was out of breath. "Look in the garden."

She went out into the garden. There was the great beanstalk, as high as the sky. And at the foot of it, on the grass, was a leather bag. She opened it. Her eyes grew very big.

"Gold!" she whispered. "Jack, it is all gold!"

"I told you that the beans were magic," said Jack. "I told you. You would not listen."

She hugged him again, and she laughed and cried together. "I am sorry that I hit you," she said. "I am so sorry. You were right, and I was wrong."

"It does not matter," said Jack. "Now we are rich."
