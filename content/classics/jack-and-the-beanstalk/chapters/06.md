# The Golden Harp

With the golden hen, Jack and his mother were never hungry again. Every morning, Jack said "Lay," and the hen laid an egg of gold. They sold the eggs in the town, and they bought a big house, with a garden and a stable. They had fine horses, warm beds, and servants to do the work. Jack's mother wore a silk dress, and she told everyone in the village that her son was a clever boy.

But Jack was not content.

He was a restless boy, and he was never happy for long. He lay in his soft bed, and he looked at the ceiling. He thought about the great white land above the clouds. He thought about the giant's house, and the long straight road, and all the wonderful things that were there. He wanted to see them again.

"I have enough," he said to himself. "I should be satisfied. But I cannot stop thinking about it. There is something else up there. I am sure of it."

At last, he made up his mind. One fine morning, he got up early, and he put on his old clothes. He climbed out of the window and on to the beanstalk, as before. He climbed and he climbed and he climbed, and he reached the top of the world.

But this time, he was more careful. He remembered that the giant's wife had seen his face twice. She would know him. She might tell her husband. He did not go straight to the house. He walked along the road until he was near it, and then he hid behind a big bush. He waited, and he watched.

After a while, the door opened, and the big woman came out. She had a pail in her hand. She walked away to the well, which was at the bottom of a little hill. As soon as she was gone, Jack ran to the house. He slipped through the open door. He crept into the kitchen.

He looked around. There was a big copper pot standing in the corner, as tall as a barrel. It had a heavy lid. Jack climbed into it, and he pulled the lid down over his head. It was dark, and it smelled of old soup. He sat very still.

He had not been there long when he heard it. Thump! Thump! Thump!

The giant and his wife came into the room.

"Fee-fi-fo-fum," said the giant. He sniffed, and he looked around. "I smell the blood of an Englishman. I smell him, wife. I smell him!"

"Do you, dear?" said the big woman. She spoke very calmly. "Then if it is that little rogue who stole your gold and your golden hen, he is surely hiding in the oven."

They both went to the oven. The giant pulled the door open and looked in. But there was nobody there. Jack was not in the oven this time.

"You see?" said the big woman. "You always say 'fee-fi-fo-fum,' and there is nobody. Of course, it is only the boy that you caught last night. I cooked him for your breakfast. How forgetful I am! And how careless you are, not to know the difference between one who is alive and one who is dead."

The giant frowned. "I could have sworn..." he said. He sat down at the table and ate. But from time to time, he muttered, "Well, I could have sworn..." and he got up to search the cupboard and the larder and under the chairs. By luck, he did not think of the copper pot.

When the giant finished his breakfast, he leaned back in his chair. "Wife," he said, "bring me my golden harp."

The woman went into the next room. She returned with a beautiful harp. It was made of gold, and it shone brighter than the sun. She put it on the table.

"Sing!" said the giant.

And the harp began to sing. Its voice was so sweet that it seemed to come from heaven. It sang of green fields and quiet rivers, of birds and of stars. Jack, in his copper pot, held his breath. He had never heard a sound so beautiful. He felt tears in his eyes.

The harp sang on and on. Slowly, the giant's eyes became heavy. His head nodded. At last, he fell asleep, and he began to snore like a thunderstorm.

Jack lifted the lid a little. He looked out. The big woman was busy in another room. The giant lay with his head on the table. The harp stood quietly in front of him, with its golden strings.

Jack climbed out of the pot, very slowly. He got down on his hands and knees. He crept across the floor, like a mouse. He came to the table. He stood up. He put his hands around the golden harp.

He took it, and he ran for the door.

But the harp was not a thing to be stolen. It was alive. It cried out in a loud voice:

"Master! Master! Master!"

The giant woke up. He jumped to his feet and turned around. He saw Jack, at the door, with his golden harp in his arms.

"THIEF!" he roared. "That is the boy! Stop him!"
