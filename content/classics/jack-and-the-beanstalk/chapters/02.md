# Five Magic Beans

The stranger was a funny-looking old man. He was short and bent, with a long grey beard and a pointed hat. His coat was green and patched, and his boots were too big for him. He leaned on a crooked stick. His eyes were small and bright, and they looked at Jack as if they knew him.

"Good morning, Jack," said the old man.

Jack stopped. "Good morning to you," he said. He wondered how the old man knew his name. "Have we met?"

"Not yet," said the old man, with a smile. "And where are you going so early, Jack? With such a fine cow?"

"I am going to the market," said Jack. "I want to sell her. She does not give milk now, and my mother says that we must sell her."

"Ah," said the old man. He walked all around the cow and looked at her. He patted her back. "A fine cow. Yes, yes. I see that she is a good cow. You look like a clever boy, Jack. I wonder if you know how many beans make five."

Jack laughed. It seemed such an easy question. "Two in each hand and one in your mouth," he said quickly.

"Right you are," said the old man. "You are as sharp as a needle. And here they are, the very beans themselves."

He put his hand in his pocket. He took out a handful of beans. They were not ordinary beans. They were strange and bright. Some were red, and some were blue, and some were yellow, and some had green and purple spots. They shone in the sun like jewels.

"Look at them," said the old man. "Have you ever seen such beans?"

"No," said Jack. He looked at them with big eyes. "They are beautiful. But they are only beans."

"Only beans!" said the old man. "Oh, no, no, no. These beans are magic. If you plant them in the evening, in the morning they will grow up to the sky."

"Really?" said Jack. "You do not say so."

"I do say so," said the old man. "And because you are so clever, I will make you an offer. I will give you these beans, and you will give me your cow."

"My cow for a handful of beans?" said Jack. He laughed again. "You must think that I am a fool!"

"I do not think that you are a fool," said the old man. "I think that you are a smart boy. I am telling you the truth. And if I am wrong, if the beans do not grow, then you may come to me and take your cow back. I promise."

Jack thought about it. He looked at the beans in the old man's hand. He looked at Milky-white. He thought of his mother, who was waiting at home for the money. He knew that he should say no. But the beans were so strange and so pretty. And the old man was so sure. He felt a small flame of excitement in his chest. What if it was true?

"If it is not true," he said, "you will give me back my cow?"

"I promise," said the old man. "Cross my heart."

"All right," said Jack. "It is a deal."

He put the rope in the old man's hand. The old man gave him the beans. They were warm and smooth, and they felt almost alive. Jack put them carefully into his pocket. He looked back once at Milky-white, and she looked at him with her big gentle eyes. Then the old man turned, and he led the cow away along the road. He walked quickly for a man so old, and in a few minutes, they were out of sight.

Jack turned around, and he walked back home. He had not gone far, so it was not yet evening when he came to the door of his house.

His mother was in the garden. She was hanging clothes on a line. When she saw him, she dropped the shirt that she was holding.

"Jack!" she cried. "Back so soon? And where is Milky-white? You sold her already? How much did you get?"

"You will never guess, Mother," said Jack. He was very proud.

"Tell me! Was it five pounds? Ten? Fifteen? No, it cannot be twenty!"

"I told you that you could not guess," said Jack. He put his hand in his pocket, and he took out the beans. He held them out. "Look! These are magic beans. If you plant them in the evening, in the morning—"

He did not finish. His mother's face went from red to white, and then to red again.

"Beans!" she cried. "BEANS! You gave away my Milky-white, the best cow in the parish, for a handful of beans? What kind of fool are you? What kind of dolt, what kind of idiot?"

"But, Mother—" said Jack.

"Take that!" she cried, and she hit him on the head with the shirt. "And that! And that!" She took the beans out of his hand, and she threw them as far as she could. They flew over the fence and fell into the long grass of the garden. "There go your precious beans! And as for you, go to bed! Not a drop to drink and not a bite to eat, this very night!"

She turned, and she went into the house. She slammed the door.

Jack stood in the garden. He did not cry, but his eyes were hot. He went up the stairs to his little room in the attic. It was small and dark. There was only a bed and a window and a little shelf. He lay on the bed, and he looked at the ceiling.

He was sad and sorry. He was sad because of his mother, who was so angry and so poor. He was sorry for himself. He was sorry because of his empty stomach, and sorry because he had been a fool. He felt that he was the stupidest boy in the world.

But at last, he was so tired that he slept.
