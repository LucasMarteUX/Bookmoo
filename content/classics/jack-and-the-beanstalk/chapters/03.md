# The Beanstalk

When Jack woke up the next morning, the room looked very strange. The sun was shining in one part of it, but the rest was dark and shady. Something was blocking the light of the window.

Jack jumped up. He put on his clothes quickly. He ran to the window and looked out.

He could not believe his eyes.

The beans that his mother threw out the day before had grown. In the night, they had put roots into the garden earth, and they had sent up a great green stalk. It was as thick as a tree. It went up, and up, and up, past the roof of the house, past the top of the old oak, past the birds and the clouds, until it was lost in the sky. Its leaves were as big as tables, and its flowers were red and gold. It twisted as it climbed, like a big plaited ladder.

"The old man told the truth," whispered Jack. "He said it, and it is true."

He opened the window. The beanstalk grew right up close to it. All he had to do was to give a jump. Jack looked down. The ground was very far away. He looked up. The top was hidden in the white mist.

"Should I?" he said to himself. "My mother would not like it. But what else can I do?"

He thought of Milky-white, and of his mother's tears, and of the empty cupboard in the kitchen. He thought of all the times that he had said, "I will do something great." This was a chance. He took a deep breath, and he jumped.

He caught hold of a thick leaf, and he found a place for his feet. It was easy. The stalk was like a ladder, with many places to hold on. Jack began to climb.

He climbed, and he climbed, and he climbed. He passed the roof of his house, and he passed the tops of the trees. The village became small. The fields looked like green and yellow squares. He climbed through a wet white cloud, and for a while, he could see nothing. Then he came out into the light, and the sun was shining again, and the sky was blue.

He climbed and he climbed. His hands were sore, and his arms hurt. But he did not stop. At last, he put his hand on something that was not a leaf. It was a flat, solid surface. He pulled himself up and over the edge.

He was standing in a strange country. It was a flat, wide land, above the clouds. The ground was soft and white, like cotton. The sky was very close. In front of him, there was a long, broad road that went as straight as an arrow into the distance.

"Well," said Jack. "Here I am. I wonder where this road goes."

He began to walk. He walked and he walked and he walked. The road was straight and smooth, and the air was clear and sweet. The sun was warm on his back. He was very hungry, because he had eaten nothing since the day before, and he began to think about his mother's bread.

At last, he saw something at the end of the road. It was a great, big, tall house. It was as tall as a church. It had a roof like a hill and a door like a barn. It stood alone, in the middle of the white land, and a thin smoke rose from its chimney.

On the doorstep, there was a great, big, tall woman.

She was as tall as two men. She had big arms, a big face, and a big apron. She was sweeping the step with a broom as big as a tree. Jack walked up to her, and he took off his cap. He was a polite boy, when he wanted to be.

"Good morning, ma'am," he said. "Would you be so kind as to give me some breakfast? I have not eaten since yesterday morning."

The woman looked down at him. She put her hands on her hips.

"Breakfast?" she said, in a deep, loud voice. "Breakfast, is it? You will be breakfast if you do not go away from here! My husband is a giant, and there is nothing that he likes better than boys, cooked on toast. You had better move on, little boy. He will be home soon."

"Oh, please, ma'am," said Jack. He put his hand on his stomach. "Please give me something to eat. I have not had a bite for a whole day. I may as well be cooked as die of hunger."

The big woman looked at him for a long moment. She was not a bad woman, after all. She did not like to see a poor boy so thin and hungry. She sighed.

"Come in, then," she said. "But be quick, and be quiet. If my husband comes home, I do not know what I will do."

She took him into the kitchen. It was a huge room. The table was as high as a house, and the chairs were like towers. She cut a big slice of bread and a piece of cheese, and she gave him a jug of milk. Jack sat on the floor, and he began to eat.
