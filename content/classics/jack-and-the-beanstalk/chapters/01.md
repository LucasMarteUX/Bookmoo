# Jack and the Cow Named Milky-White

Once upon a time, there was a poor widow. She lived in a little house at the edge of a village, and she had an only son named Jack. They had no money, no land, and no servants. All that they had was a small garden, a thin roof, and a cow named Milky-white.

Milky-white was a good cow. She was white and brown, with big gentle eyes. Every morning, she gave a pail of milk. Jack's mother took the milk to the market in the village, and she sold it for a few coins. With the coins, she bought bread and a little cheese. This was how they lived. They did not have much, but they were not hungry.

Jack was a tall, thin boy with untidy hair and a quick smile. He was a good-natured boy, and he loved his mother and his cow. But he was also very lazy. He did not like to work. He liked to lie under a tree and look at the clouds, and he liked to dream of the great things that he would do one day. When his mother asked him to fetch water or chop wood, he always said, "In a minute." And the minute was always a very long time.

"Jack," said his mother, "you are a big boy now. You must help me. You are sixteen years old. In my day, a boy of sixteen worked from morning to night."

"I will, Mother," said Jack. "I will do something great. You will see."

"I would rather see you do something small," said his mother, "but something real."

One morning, Jack went to the shed to bring Milky-white to the pail. He sat down on his little stool, and he waited for the milk. But no milk came. He tried again. Nothing. He looked into the pail. It was empty.

"Milky-white," said Jack. "What is the matter with you? Are you ill?"

The cow turned her head and looked at him. She did not seem ill. But she gave no milk, not that morning, and not the next. After three days, Jack's mother came out to the shed and looked at the cow herself. Her face was white.

"She is old," said the widow. She sat down on a stone, and she put her face in her hands. "She is too old to give milk. What shall we do? What shall we do?"

"I will find work," said Jack. "I will go to the town, and I will ask the farmers. Somebody will give me a job."

"We have tried that before," said his mother, with tears in her eyes. "Nobody wanted you. They said that you are too young, or too lazy, or too dreamy. No, Jack. There is only one thing to do. We must sell Milky-white. We will take the money, and we will buy something. Perhaps we will start a little shop. Perhaps we will find a better cow. But we cannot keep her here, when she gives no milk."

Jack looked at the cow. His heart was heavy. He loved Milky-white. But he knew that his mother was right.

"All right," he said. "Today is market day. I will take her to the town, and I will sell her. I will get a good price. You will see."

"Do not let anyone cheat you," said his mother. "Ask for at least five pounds."

"I will, Mother."

Jack put a rope around the cow's neck. His mother kissed him on the cheek, and she gave him a piece of bread and an apple for his lunch. Then she stood at the door and waved, with her hand over her eyes.

Jack walked along the road, and Milky-white walked behind him. It was a bright morning, and the road was dry. He whistled as he went. He was proud to be doing such an important job. He thought, "I will be a rich man. I will buy my mother a new dress. I will buy a farm, and a horse."

He had not walked very far when he met a stranger on the road.
