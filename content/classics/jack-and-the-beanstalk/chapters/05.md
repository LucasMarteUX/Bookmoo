# The Hen That Laid Golden Eggs

For some time, Jack and his mother lived well on the bag of gold. They bought a new cow, and a cart, and good food and warm clothes. They mended the roof. They had meat on the table every day. Jack's mother sang while she cooked. She said that she had never been so happy.

But gold does not last forever. A bag, however heavy, grows lighter with every coin. At last, there was only a handful left, and then there was nothing at all.

"Mother," said Jack one evening, "we have no more money."

"No," she said. She looked sad. "I was afraid of that. We will have to go back to our old ways. But I do not mind. We have lived well for a while."

"I do not want to go back to the old ways," said Jack. "I will try my luck again. I will go up the beanstalk."

"Jack, no!" said his mother. "It is too dangerous. You might be caught!"

"I will be careful," said Jack. "I know the road now. I know the house. I will be quick."

His mother argued, but it was no use. Jack had made up his mind. The next morning, he got up very early, before the sun. He put some bread in his pocket, and he climbed out of the window and on to the beanstalk. It was still there, strong and green. He climbed and he climbed, and he came at last to the white land at the top.

He walked along the long straight road, and he came to the great, big, tall house. The big woman was on the doorstep, as before. She was shaking a rug. Jack took off his cap.

"Good morning, ma'am," he said, as boldly as he could. "Could you be so kind as to give me something to eat?"

The woman looked at him. She put down her rug.

"Go away, my boy," she said. "My husband is not far, and he will eat you for breakfast. But wait. Are you not the boy who came here once before? Do you know, on that very day, my husband found that one of his bags of gold was missing?"

"That is strange, ma'am," said Jack. "I dare say I could tell you something about that. But I am so hungry that I cannot speak until I have eaten."

The big woman was so curious that she let him in. She gave him a bowl of soup and a big piece of bread. But Jack had hardly started to eat, when thump! thump! thump! the house began to shake.

"It is my husband!" cried the woman. "Quick!" And she hid Jack in the oven again.

Everything happened as before. The giant came in, with three oxen hanging from his belt. He threw them on the table. He stopped and sniffed, and he said:

"Fee-fi-fo-fum,
I smell the blood of an Englishman!"

"Nonsense, dear," said his wife. "It is only the smell of your breakfast." And she cooked three oxen, and the giant ate them all.

"Now, wife," said the giant, when he was done. "Bring me my hen that lays the golden eggs."

The woman went to a shelf, and she took down a small hen. It was not a big hen. It was brown and plain, like any hen in a farmyard. She put it on the table in front of the giant.

"Lay!" said the giant.

The hen shook its feathers. It made a noise: cluck, cluck, cluck. And on the table, in front of the giant, there was a golden egg. It was round and bright, and it shone in the light like a little sun.

Jack watched through the crack of the oven door. His mouth opened. "A hen that lays golden eggs," he whispered. "I must have it."

The giant took the golden egg, and he put it in his pocket. "Lay!" he said again, and a second egg appeared. He did this until he had a pile of eggs on the table. Then he began to nod. His head went down, and his eyes closed. In a few minutes, he was snoring like thunder.

Jack opened the oven door. He crept out very, very quietly. The big woman was in the next room. He stood on tiptoe. He put out his hands, and he took the hen. It was warm and soft. It did not move. He held it under his arm, and he walked carefully to the door.

But at the last moment, the hen gave a loud cackle.

"CLUCK! CLUCK! CLUCK!"

The giant woke up with a roar.

"Wife! Wife!" he shouted. "What have you done with my golden hen?"

"What, dear?" said the woman, from the next room.

But Jack did not wait to hear more. He ran out of the door, and he ran down the long straight road as fast as he could. He did not look back. He came to the beanstalk, and he went down like a house on fire. His hands burned on the leaves. He fell the last part, and he landed in the garden with a bump.

His mother ran out. "Jack!" she cried. "What have you got there?"

"Look, Mother," said Jack. He held out the hen. "Say 'Lay.'"

His mother looked at him as if he was mad. "Lay," she said.

The hen shook itself, and it laid a golden egg on the grass.
