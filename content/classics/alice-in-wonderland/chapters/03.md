# The Caucus-Race and the Long Tale

"Perhaps this mouse can talk," Alice thought. "Everything is so strange down here. Maybe it can." So she spoke to it. "Oh Mouse, do you know the way out of this pool? I am very tired of swimming."

The Mouse looked at her carefully. It seemed to wink with one small eye, but it said nothing.

"Perhaps it does not understand English," thought Alice. "Maybe it is a French mouse, from the time of William the Conqueror." (Alice knew some history, but she was not sure about dates.) So she began again. "Où est ma chatte?" That was the first sentence in her French book. It means, "Where is my cat?"

The Mouse jumped out of the water. It shook all over with fear. "Oh, I am so sorry!" cried Alice quickly. "I forgot that you do not like cats."

"I do not like cats!" shouted the Mouse in a high, angry voice. "Would you like cats, if you were me?"

"Well, perhaps not," said Alice gently. "Please do not be angry. But I wish that I could show you my cat, Dinah. I think that you would like cats if you saw her. She is so quiet and sweet. She sits by the fire and washes her face. She is so soft to hold. And she is very good at catching mice. Oh, I am sorry!" The Mouse was shaking with anger now, and all its fur stood up. "We will not talk about her again."

"We, indeed!" cried the Mouse. "My family always hated cats. Nasty, low, ugly things! Do not say that word again!"

"I will not!" said Alice, and she quickly changed the subject. "Are you... are you fond of... of dogs?" The Mouse did not answer, so she went on. "There is a lovely little dog near our house. It has bright eyes and long brown curly hair. It will bring back things that you throw. It sits up and begs for its dinner. It belongs to a farmer, who says that it kills all the rats and... oh dear!" cried Alice sadly. "I have made it angry again!" The Mouse was swimming away from her as fast as it could. The water moved all around it.

So she called to it softly. "Dear Mouse, please come back. We will not talk about cats or dogs if you do not like them." When the Mouse heard this, it turned around and swam slowly back. Its face was quite white. "Let us get to the shore," it said in a low voice, "and then I will tell you my story. You will understand why I hate cats and dogs."

It was time to go. The pool was getting crowded with birds and animals that had fallen in. There was a Duck and a Dodo, a Lory and an Eaglet, and some other strange creatures. Alice led the way, and the whole group swam to the shore.

They were a very strange group on the bank. The birds had wet feathers. The animals had wet fur. They were all dripping, cross and uncomfortable.

The first question was: how do we get dry? They had a meeting about it. After a few minutes, it seemed quite natural to Alice to talk to them as if she knew them all her life. She even had a long argument with the Lory, who said, "I am older than you, so I know better." Alice would not agree without knowing the Lory's age. The Lory refused to tell her, so there was nothing more to say.

At last, the Mouse called out, "Sit down, everybody, and listen to me! I will soon make you dry!" They all sat down in a big circle, with the Mouse in the middle. Alice watched it nervously. She was sure that she would catch a bad cold if she did not get dry soon.

"Ahem!" said the Mouse, in an important voice. "Are you all ready? This is the driest thing that I know. Quiet, please! William the Conqueror won England with the help of the Pope. The English people needed a leader, and they were used to being conquered..."

"Ugh!" said the Lory, shaking.

"I beg your pardon," said the Mouse, frowning, but very politely. "Did you say something?"

"Not me!" said the Lory quickly.

"I thought you did. I will go on. 'Edwin and Morcar, the earls of two counties, supported him.'"

"Supported what?" asked the Duck.

"Supported him," said the Mouse, a little cross. "You know what 'him' means."

"I know what 'it' means when I find something," said the Duck. "It is usually a frog or a worm. The question is, what did the bishop find?"

The Mouse did not hear this. It went on quickly. But the long story did not make anyone dry. "How are you now, my dear?" the Mouse asked Alice.

"Wet, as always," said Alice sadly. "That does not dry me at all."

"In that case," said the Dodo, standing up very seriously, "I suggest that we end the meeting. We need a better plan."

"Speak English!" said the Eaglet. "I do not understand half of those long words. And I do not think that you understand them either!" The Eaglet bent its head to hide a smile. Some other birds laughed loudly.

"What I wanted to say," said the Dodo, a little offended, "was that the best way to get dry is a Caucus-race."

"What is a Caucus-race?" asked Alice. She did not really want to know. But the Dodo waited, as if someone should speak, and nobody else said anything.

"The best way to explain it," said the Dodo, "is to do it." (And in case you want to try it on a cold winter day, I will tell you how the Dodo did it.)

First, he drew a circle on the ground for the race-track. ("The exact shape is not important," he said.) Then he put everybody along the track, here and there. There was no "One, two, three, go!" They began to run when they wanted, and they stopped when they wanted. So it was hard to know when the race ended. But after half an hour, they were all dry again. Suddenly, the Dodo shouted, "The race is over!" They all crowded around him, out of breath, and asked, "But who won?"

The Dodo could not answer without thinking a lot. He sat for a long time with one finger on his forehead. The others waited quietly. At last, he said, "Everybody has won, and everybody must have a prize."

"But who will give the prizes?" they all asked at once.

"She will, of course," said the Dodo, pointing at Alice. At once, they all crowded around her. "Prizes! Prizes!" they shouted.

Alice did not know what to do. She put her hand in her pocket, and she found a box of sweets. (Luckily, the salt water had not got into it.) She gave one sweet to each animal. There was exactly one for everybody.

"But she must have a prize too," said the Mouse.

"Of course," said the Dodo seriously. "What else do you have in your pocket?" he asked Alice.

"Only a thimble," said Alice sadly.

"Give it to me," said the Dodo.

Then they all crowded around her again. The Dodo held out the thimble and said, "We ask you to accept this beautiful thimble." They all cheered.

Alice thought that it was all very silly. But they looked so serious that she did not dare to laugh. She could not think of anything to say. So she bowed and took the thimble, and she tried to look as serious as she could.

Then they ate the sweets. There was some noise and trouble. The big birds said that they could not taste theirs. The small birds choked, and someone had to hit them on the back. But it was over at last. They sat down in a circle again and asked the Mouse to tell them something more.

"You promised to tell me your story," said Alice. "And why you hate C and D," she added in a whisper. She was afraid that it would be angry again.

"Mine is a long and sad tale!" said the Mouse, sighing.

"It is a long tail, that is true," said Alice, looking at the Mouse's tail. "But why do you say it is sad?" She kept thinking about this while the Mouse spoke. So the story that she imagined looked like this on the page, long and thin, like a tail, and getting smaller and smaller at the end.

The Mouse looked at her. "You are not listening!" it said angrily. "What are you thinking about?"

"I am sorry," said Alice humbly. "You were at the fifth bend, I think?"

"I was not!" cried the Mouse. "I will not tell you any more. You are laughing at me!" It got up and walked away.

"I did not mean to!" said poor Alice. "But you are so easily offended!"

The Mouse only growled and went on walking.

"Please come back and finish your story!" Alice called. The others called out, "Yes, please!" But the Mouse shook its head and walked faster.

"What a pity that it would not stay!" said the Lory. An old Crab said to her daughter, "Let this be a lesson to you, my dear. Never lose your temper!"

"Be quiet, Mother!" said the young Crab. "You would make an oyster lose its patience!"

"I wish that Dinah was here!" said Alice aloud. "She would soon bring it back."

"And who is Dinah, if I may ask?" said the Lory.

Alice was happy to talk about her pet. "Dinah is our cat. She is so good at catching mice, you cannot imagine! And you should see her chase the birds! She will eat a small bird as soon as look at it!"

This speech caused a great stir. Some of the birds hurried off at once. An old Magpie wrapped herself up carefully. "I must go home," she said. "The night air is bad for my throat!" A Canary called to her children in a trembling voice, "Come away, my dears! It is time for bed!" Each one found an excuse to leave. Soon Alice was alone.

"I wish that I had not talked about Dinah," she said sadly. "Nobody likes her down here. But she is the best cat in the world! Oh, Dinah, my dear! Will I ever see you again?" And poor Alice began to cry again. She felt very lonely and sad.
