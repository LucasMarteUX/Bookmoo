# Who Stole the Tarts?

The King and Queen of Hearts were sitting on their throne when Alice arrived. A big crowd stood around them. There were small birds and animals, and the whole pack of cards. The Knave was standing in front of the King and Queen, in chains. A soldier stood on each side to guard him. Near the King was the White Rabbit. He had a trumpet in one hand and a long paper in the other. In the middle of the court, there was a table with a big plate of tarts. They looked so good that Alice felt hungry. "I wish that they would finish the trial," she thought, "and give out the food!"

Alice had never been in a court, but she had read about courts in books. She was pleased that she knew the names of almost everything. "That is the judge," she thought, "because of his big wig." The judge was the King. He wore his crown on top of the wig, and he did not look comfortable at all.

"And that is the jury box," she thought. "Those twelve creatures are the jurors." There were twelve of them, some animals and some birds. They were all writing busily on small boards.

"What are they doing?" Alice whispered to the Gryphon. "They cannot write anything yet. The trial has not started."

"They are writing their names," the Gryphon whispered, "in case they forget them before the end."

"Silence in the court!" cried the White Rabbit. The King put on his glasses and looked around to see who was talking.

The King said, "Read the accusation!"

The White Rabbit blew his trumpet three times. Then he opened the paper and read:

"The Queen of Hearts, she made some tarts,
All on a summer day.
The Knave of Hearts, he stole those tarts,
And took them quite away!"

"Think about your verdict," the King said to the jury.

"Not yet, not yet!" said the Rabbit quickly. "There is a lot to do first!"

"Call the first witness," said the King. The White Rabbit blew the trumpet three times and called, "First witness!"

The first witness was the Hatter. He came in with a teacup in one hand and a piece of bread and butter in the other. "I am sorry, Your Majesty," he began, "that I bring these in. But I had not finished my tea when they called me."

"You should have finished," said the King. "When did you start?"

The Hatter looked at the March Hare, who had followed him arm in arm with the Dormouse. "The fourteenth of March, I think," he said.

"Give your evidence," said the King, "and do not be nervous, or I will have you killed on the spot."

This did not help the witness. He moved from one foot to the other and looked nervously at the Queen. In his fear, he bit a big piece out of his teacup instead of the bread and butter.

At that moment, Alice felt a strange feeling. She did not understand it at first. Then she saw what it was. She was growing bigger again. First, she thought that she should get up and leave the court. But then she decided to stay as long as there was room.

"Please do not push so much," said the Dormouse, who sat next to her. "I can hardly breathe."

"I cannot help it," said Alice. "I am growing."

"You have no right to grow here," said the Dormouse.

"Do not talk nonsense," said Alice, more boldly. "You are growing too."

"Yes, but I grow slowly," said the Dormouse. "Not in that silly way." And he got up in a bad mood and went to the other side of the court.

The Hatter's evidence was not useful, and the cook's was no better. At last, the White Rabbit called the next witness. What was the name? "Alice!"

"Here!" cried Alice. She forgot how big she had become in the last few minutes. She jumped up so fast that her skirt hit the jury box and tipped it over. All the jurors fell on the heads of the crowd below. They lay there, moving their arms and legs. They reminded her of the goldfish that she had spilled by accident the week before.

"Oh, I am so sorry!" she said, very upset. She began to pick them up quickly. She kept thinking of the goldfish. She had a feeling that she must put them back in the box at once, or they would die.

"The trial cannot go on," said the King in a very serious voice, "until all the jurors are back in their places. All of them," he said again, looking hard at Alice.

Soon everything was ready again. The King said, "What do you know about this?"

"Nothing," said Alice.

"Nothing at all?" said the King.

"Nothing at all," said Alice.

"That is very important," said the King, and he turned to the jury. They began to write it down. But the White Rabbit said quickly, "Not important, Your Majesty means, of course." He spoke politely, but he made faces at the King.

"Not important, of course, I meant," said the King. Then he said to himself, "important, not important, not important, important," as if he wanted to find out which word sounded better.

Then the King, who was writing in his notebook, called, "Silence!" He read from the book, "Rule forty-two. Anyone more than a mile high must leave the court."

Everybody looked at Alice.

"I am not a mile high," said Alice.

"You are," said the King.

"Nearly two miles," said the Queen.

"Well, I will not go," said Alice. "And that is not a real rule. You just invented it."

"It is the oldest rule in the book," said the King.

"Then it should be number one," said Alice.

The King turned pale and closed his notebook quickly. "Think about your verdict," he said to the jury, in a low and shaking voice.

"There is more evidence, Your Majesty," said the White Rabbit, and he jumped up. "This paper has just been found. It looks like a letter from the prisoner to... to somebody."

Nobody could understand the letter. At last, the Queen shouted, "Sentence first, verdict afterwards!"

"Stuff and nonsense!" said Alice loudly. "What an idea, to have the sentence first!"

"Be quiet!" said the Queen. Her face was purple.

"I will not!" said Alice.

"Off with her head!" the Queen shouted at the top of her voice. Nobody moved.

"Who cares about you?" said Alice. She had now grown to her full size. "You are nothing but a pack of cards!"

At these words, the whole pack of cards rose in the air and came flying down on her. She gave a small scream, half in fear and half in anger, and tried to push them away. And then she was lying on the river bank, with her head in her sister's lap. Her sister was gently brushing some dead leaves from her face.

"Wake up, Alice dear!" said her sister. "What a long sleep you had!"

"Oh, I had such a strange dream!" said Alice. She told her sister, as well as she could remember, all the strange adventures that you have just read about. When she finished, her sister kissed her. "It was a strange dream, dear," she said. "But now run in for your tea. It is getting late."

Alice got up and ran off. As she ran, she thought about what a wonderful dream it was.

But her sister stayed where she was. She leaned her head on her hand, watched the setting sun, and thought about little Alice and her wonderful adventures. She also dreamed in her own way. First, she dreamed about little Alice. She saw the small hands on her knee, and the bright, eager eyes looking up at her. She could hear Alice's voice and see her little head moving to keep the hair out of her eyes. And as she listened, the whole place around her became alive with the strange creatures from her sister's dream.
