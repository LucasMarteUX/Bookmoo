# Advice from a Caterpillar

Alice walked through the wood for a while. She was still very small, and the trees and the flowers looked as tall as a forest. She had two things on her mind. First, she had to grow to the right size again. Second, she wanted to find the way into the lovely garden.

"It sounds easy," she said to herself. "But how do I do it? I think that I must eat or drink something. But what?"

She looked around at the flowers and the grass, but she saw nothing to eat or drink. There was a big mushroom near her, about as tall as herself. She looked under it, and on both sides of it, and behind it. Then she thought that she should look on top.

She stood on her toes and looked over the edge. Her eyes met the eyes of a large blue caterpillar. He was sitting on the top with his arms folded, quietly smoking a long pipe. He did not look at her at all.

For a long time, the Caterpillar and Alice looked at each other without a word. At last, the Caterpillar took the pipe out of his mouth and spoke in a slow, sleepy voice.

"Who are you?" he said.

It was not a friendly start. Alice answered shyly, "I do not know, sir, just now. I knew who I was when I got up this morning. But I think that I have changed several times since then."

"What do you mean?" said the Caterpillar sharply. "Explain yourself!"

"I cannot explain myself, sir," said Alice. "I am not myself, you see."

"I do not see," said the Caterpillar.

"I cannot say it more clearly," Alice said politely. "I do not understand it myself. And it is very confusing to be so many sizes in one day."

"It is not," said the Caterpillar.

"Well, maybe you have not felt it yet," said Alice. "But one day, you will turn into a chrysalis, and then into a butterfly. I think that you will feel a little strange then, will you not?"

"Not at all," said the Caterpillar.

"Perhaps your feelings are different," said Alice. "I only know that it would feel strange to me."

"You!" said the Caterpillar. "Who are you?"

This brought them back to the start of their talk. Alice felt a little annoyed by his short answers. She stood up straight and said seriously, "I think that you should tell me who you are first."

"Why?" said the Caterpillar.

That was another difficult question. Alice could not think of a good reason. The Caterpillar seemed to be in a bad mood. So she turned away.

"Come back!" the Caterpillar called. "I have something important to say!"

That sounded good. Alice turned and came back.

"Keep your temper," said the Caterpillar.

"Is that all?" said Alice. She tried not to be angry.

"No," said the Caterpillar.

Alice decided to wait. She had nothing else to do, and maybe he would say something useful. For some minutes, he smoked and said nothing. At last, he unfolded his arms, took the pipe out of his mouth and said, "So you think that you are changed, do you?"

"I am afraid so, sir," said Alice. "I cannot remember things as I did. And I do not stay the same size for ten minutes!"

"What things can you not remember?"

"I tried to say a poem about a busy little bee, but it came out all different," said Alice in a sad voice.

"Say 'You are old, Father William,'" said the Caterpillar.

Alice folded her hands and began:

"You are old, Father William," the young man said,
"And your hair has become very white.
And yet you stand on your head all the time.
Do you think, at your age, it is right?"

"When I was young," Father William replied,
"I was afraid that it could hurt my brain.
But now that I know I have none at all,
Why, I do it again and again."

"That is not right," said the Caterpillar.

"Not quite right, I am afraid," said Alice shyly. "Some of the words are different."

"It is wrong from beginning to end," said the Caterpillar. They were silent for some minutes.

The Caterpillar spoke first. "What size do you want to be?" he asked.

"Oh, I do not mind," Alice said quickly. "But it is not nice to change so often, you know."

"I do not know," said the Caterpillar.

Alice said nothing. Nobody had ever argued with her so much. She felt that she was losing her temper.

"Are you happy now?" said the Caterpillar.

"I would like to be a little taller, sir, if you do not mind," said Alice. "Three inches is a terrible height."

"It is a very good height!" said the Caterpillar angrily. He stood up straight as he spoke. (He was exactly three inches tall.)

"But I am not used to it!" said poor Alice sadly. She thought, "I wish that these creatures were not so easily offended!"

"You will get used to it," said the Caterpillar. He put the pipe in his mouth and started smoking again.

This time, Alice waited patiently. After a minute or two, the Caterpillar took the pipe out, yawned and shook himself. He got down off the mushroom and crawled away into the grass. As he went, he said only, "One side will make you taller, and the other side will make you shorter."

"One side of what? The other side of what?" thought Alice.

"Of the mushroom," said the Caterpillar, as if she had asked aloud. A moment later, he was out of sight.

Alice looked at the mushroom for a minute and tried to see the two sides. But it was perfectly round, so it was a very hard question. At last, she put her arms around it as far as they could go. She broke off a piece of the edge with each hand.

"Now, which is which?" she said. She tried a little of the right-hand piece. At once, she felt a hard blow under her chin. Her chin had hit her foot!

She was very frightened by this sudden change. She was shrinking fast, and there was no time to lose. So she quickly began to eat some of the other piece. Her chin was pressed so tight against her foot that she could hardly open her mouth. But she did it at last, and she swallowed a small bit of the left-hand piece.

"My head is free at last!" said Alice happily. But her joy changed to fear. Where were her shoulders? She looked down. All that she could see was a very long neck. It looked like a stem that rose out of a sea of green leaves far below.

"What is all that green stuff?" said Alice. "And where are my shoulders? Oh, my poor hands, why can I not see you?" She was moving them as she spoke, but only the green leaves far away moved a little.

A big pigeon flew into her face and hit her hard with its wings. "Snake!" it screamed. "I have tried everywhere, and nothing works for them!"

"I am not a snake!" said Alice angrily. "Leave me alone!"

At last, she carefully ate a little of the right-hand piece again. After a while, she was back to her proper height. It was so long since she had been the right size that it felt strange at first. But she got used to it in a few minutes, and she began to talk to herself, as always. "Now half of my plan is done! How confusing all these changes are! I never know what I will be from one minute to the next. But I am my right size again. Now I must get into that beautiful garden. How can I do it?"
