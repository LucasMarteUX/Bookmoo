# Pig and Pepper

For a minute or two, Alice looked at a little house in the middle of the wood. She did not know what to do next. Suddenly, a man in a uniform ran out of the trees. She thought that he was a servant, because of the uniform. (But he had a face like a fish.) He knocked loudly on the door. It was opened by another servant in uniform. This one had a round face and big eyes like a frog. Both men had powdered hair that curled all over their heads. Alice was curious, so she came a little out of the wood to listen.

The Fish-Servant took a big letter from under his arm. It was almost as big as himself. He gave it to the other and said in a serious voice, "For the Duchess. An invitation from the Queen to play croquet." The Frog-Servant said the same words in the same serious voice, but in a different order. "From the Queen. An invitation for the Duchess to play croquet." Then they both bowed low, and their curls got tangled.

Alice laughed so much that she had to run back into the trees. When she looked out again, the Fish-Servant was gone. The other was sitting on the ground by the door, staring up at the sky like a fool.

Alice went shyly to the door and knocked.

"It is no use knocking," said the servant. "There are two reasons. First, I am on the same side of the door as you. Second, they are making so much noise inside that nobody can hear you." It was true. There was a terrible noise inside: someone was crying and sneezing, and now and then, there was a crash, as if a plate had broken.

"Please," said Alice, "how can I get in?"

"It would make some sense to knock," said the servant, without looking at her, "if the door was between us. For example, if you were inside, you could knock, and I could let you out." He was looking up at the sky all the time. Alice thought that this was very rude. "But maybe he cannot help it," she thought. "His eyes are so near the top of his head. Still, he could answer my question. How can I get in?" she said again.

"I will sit here," said the servant, "until tomorrow."

At that moment, the door opened, and a big plate flew out. It went straight at the servant's head. It touched his nose and broke on a tree behind him.

"Or until the day after tomorrow, perhaps," the servant said in the same voice, as if nothing happened.

"How can I get in?" asked Alice again, louder.

"Should you get in at all?" said the servant. "That is the first question."

It was, of course. But Alice did not like to hear it. "It is terrible," she said to herself, "how all these creatures argue. It makes me crazy!"

The servant thought that it was a good time to repeat himself. "I will sit here, on and off, for days and days."

"But what must I do?" said Alice.

"Anything you like," said the servant, and he began to whistle.

"Oh, it is no use talking to him," said Alice. "He is completely stupid!" And she opened the door and went in.

The door led straight into a big kitchen. It was full of smoke from one end to the other. The Duchess was sitting on a stool with three legs in the middle of the room. She was holding a baby. The cook was standing at the fire and stirring a big pot of soup.

"There is too much pepper in that soup!" Alice said to herself. She could hardly speak, because she was sneezing so much. There was too much pepper in the air, too. Even the Duchess sneezed sometimes. The baby was sneezing and crying all the time. Only two creatures in the kitchen did not sneeze: the cook, and a big cat that sat by the fire and smiled from ear to ear.

"Please tell me," said Alice, a little shyly, because she was not sure if it was polite to speak first, "why does your cat smile like that?"

"It is a Cheshire cat," said the Duchess, "and that is why. Pig!"

She said the last word so suddenly that Alice jumped. But then she saw that the Duchess was talking to the baby, not to her. So she took courage and went on.

"I did not know that Cheshire cats always smile. I did not know that cats could smile."

"They all can," said the Duchess. "And most of them do."

"I do not know any that do," said Alice politely. She was pleased to be having a conversation.

"You do not know much," said the Duchess. "That is a fact."

Alice did not like this. She wanted to talk about something else. While she was thinking, the cook took the pot off the fire. At once, she began to throw things at the Duchess and the baby. First came the fire tools. Then came a shower of pans, plates and dishes. The Duchess did not notice them, even when they hit her. The baby was already crying so loudly that nobody could tell if the things hurt it.

"Oh, please be careful!" cried Alice, jumping up and down in fear. "Oh, there goes his nose!" A very big pan flew past and nearly took the baby's nose with it.

"If everyone minded their own business," said the Duchess in a rough voice, "the world would go around much faster."

Then she began to sing to the baby. After every line, she gave it a hard shake. At last, she threw it to Alice. "Here! You can hold it for a while, if you like," she said. "I must go and get ready to play croquet with the Queen." And she hurried out of the room.

The baby made a noise like a pig. Alice carried it out into the fresh air. "If I do not take this child with me," she thought, "they will kill it in a day or two. It would be murder to leave it here." The baby grunted again. Alice looked at its face. It was a strange face, with a nose that turned up like a pig's snout. Its eyes were very small for a baby. Alice did not like the look of it at all.

"Do not make that noise," said Alice. "It is not a good way to talk."

The baby grunted again, and she looked closely at its face. There was no doubt. It had a pig's nose. It was a pig! She knew that it was silly to carry it any further. So she put it down, and she was relieved to see it trot quietly away into the wood.

Suddenly, Alice saw the Cheshire Cat. It was sitting on a branch of a tree, a few yards away. The Cat grinned when it saw her. It looked friendly, she thought. But it had very long claws and a lot of teeth, so she decided to be polite.

"Cheshire Puss," she began, a little shyly, "could you tell me which way to go from here?"

"That depends on where you want to go," said the Cat.

"I do not care where," said Alice.

"Then it does not matter which way you go," said the Cat.

"As long as I get somewhere," Alice added.

"Oh, you will do that," said the Cat, "if you walk long enough."

Alice could not argue with that. So she asked another question. "What kind of people live around here?"

"In that direction," said the Cat, waving its right paw, "lives a Hatter. In that direction," waving the other paw, "lives a March Hare. You can visit either. They are both mad."

"But I do not want to go to mad people," said Alice.

"Oh, you cannot help that," said the Cat. "We are all mad here. I am mad. You are mad."

"How do you know that I am mad?" said Alice.

"You must be," said the Cat, "or you would not be here."

And then it disappeared slowly. First, the end of its tail went. Then the rest of it went, and the smile stayed last. The big smile stayed in the air for some time after the rest of the Cat was gone.
