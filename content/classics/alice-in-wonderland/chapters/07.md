# A Mad Tea-Party

There was a table under a tree in front of the house. The March Hare and the Hatter were having tea at it. A Dormouse was sitting between them, fast asleep. The other two used it as a cushion. They rested their elbows on it and talked over its head. "That must be uncomfortable for the Dormouse," thought Alice. "But it is asleep, so maybe it does not mind."

The table was big, but the three of them sat close together at one corner. "No room! No room!" they cried when they saw Alice.

"There is plenty of room!" said Alice angrily. She sat down in a big armchair at the end of the table.

"Have some wine," said the March Hare in a friendly way.

Alice looked all around the table. There was only tea. "I do not see any wine," she said.

"There is none," said the March Hare.

"Then it was not polite of you to offer it," said Alice.

"It was not polite of you to sit down without an invitation," said the March Hare.

"I did not know that it was your table," said Alice. "It is set for many more than three."

"Your hair needs cutting," said the Hatter. He had been looking at Alice for some time, and this was his first remark.

"You should not make personal remarks," said Alice seriously. "It is very rude."

The Hatter opened his eyes wide. But all he said was, "Why is a raven like a writing-desk?"

"Good, now we will have some fun!" thought Alice. "I am glad that they are asking riddles. I think that I can guess that one," she said aloud.

"Do you mean that you can find the answer?" said the March Hare.

"Yes," said Alice.

"Then you should say what you mean," said the March Hare.

"I do," said Alice quickly. "At least, I mean what I say. That is the same thing."

"It is not the same thing at all!" said the Hatter. "You could say that 'I see what I eat' is the same as 'I eat what I see'!"

"And you could say," said the March Hare, "that 'I like what I get' is the same as 'I get what I like'!"

"And you could say," said the Dormouse, who seemed to be talking in its sleep, "that 'I breathe when I sleep' is the same as 'I sleep when I breathe'!"

"For you, it is the same," said the Hatter. After that, nobody spoke for a minute. Alice thought about ravens and writing-desks, but she did not know much about them.

The Hatter spoke first. "What day of the month is it?" he asked Alice. He had taken his watch out of his pocket. He was looking at it nervously, shaking it, and listening to it.

Alice thought for a moment. "The fourth," she said.

"Two days wrong!" said the Hatter, with a sigh. "I told you that butter was bad for the watch!" He looked angrily at the March Hare.

"It was the best butter," said the March Hare quietly.

"Yes, but some crumbs went in too," the Hatter said. "You should not have put it in with the bread knife."

The March Hare took the watch and looked at it sadly. Then he put it in his cup of tea and looked at it again. He could only say, "It was the best butter, you know."

Alice looked over his shoulder with some interest. "What a funny watch!" she said. "It tells the day of the month, but not the time!"

"Why should it?" said the Hatter. "Does your watch tell you the year?"

"Of course not," said Alice. "But that is because the year stays the same for so long."

"That is exactly the case with mine," said the Hatter.

Alice felt very confused. The Hatter's words had no meaning, and yet it was English. "I do not understand you," she said, as politely as she could.

"The Dormouse is asleep again," said the Hatter, and he poured a little hot tea on its nose.

The Dormouse shook its head and said, without opening its eyes, "Of course, of course. I was going to say that myself."

"Have you guessed the riddle?" the Hatter asked Alice.

"No, I give up," said Alice. "What is the answer?"

"I have no idea," said the Hatter.

"Neither have I," said the March Hare.

Alice sighed. "I think that you could use your time better," she said, "than to ask riddles without answers."

"If you knew Time as well as I do," said the Hatter, "you would not say 'waste it.' It is him."

"I do not know what you mean," said Alice.

"Of course not!" said the Hatter, and he tossed his head. "I am sure that you never spoke to Time!"

"Maybe not," said Alice. "But I know that I must keep time when I learn music."

"Ah, that explains it!" said the Hatter. "He does not like to be kept. If you were friends with him, he would do almost anything with the clock. For example, it is nine o'clock in the morning, and lessons begin. You only whisper a word to Time, and the clock goes round in a second! Half past one, time for lunch!"

("I wish that it was," the March Hare whispered to himself.)

"That would be wonderful," said Alice. "But then I would not be hungry yet."

"Not at first," said the Hatter. "But you could keep it at half past one as long as you liked."

"Is that how you do it?" asked Alice.

The Hatter shook his head sadly. "Not me!" he said. "We had a quarrel last March, just before he went mad." (He pointed his spoon at the March Hare.) "It was at the big concert of the Queen of Hearts. I had to sing 'Twinkle, twinkle, little bat! How I wonder what you are at!' Do you know the song?"

"I know something like it," said Alice.

"It goes on like this," said the Hatter. "'Up above the world you fly, like a tea tray in the sky. Twinkle, twinkle...'"

The Dormouse woke up and started to sing in its sleep. "Twinkle, twinkle, twinkle, twinkle..." It went on for so long that they had to pinch it to make it stop.

"Well," said the Hatter, "I had hardly finished the first verse, when the Queen jumped up. She shouted, 'He is killing the time! Off with his head!'"

"How terrible!" said Alice.

"And since then," said the Hatter sadly, "Time will not do anything that I ask. It is always six o'clock now."

A bright idea came to Alice. "Is that why there are so many tea things on the table?" she asked.

"Yes," said the Hatter, with a sigh. "It is always tea time, and there is no time to wash the cups."

"So you move around the table?" said Alice.

"Yes," said the Hatter. "When the things are used."

"But what happens when you come back to the beginning?" Alice asked.

"Let us change the subject," said the March Hare, yawning. "I am tired of this."

Alice was too confused to say any more. After a while, she stood up and walked away in disgust. The Dormouse fell asleep again at once. The others did not notice her go. She looked back once or twice, and she hoped that they would call her. The last time that she saw them, they were trying to put the Dormouse into the teapot.

"I will never go there again!" said Alice, as she walked through the wood. "It is the stupidest tea party that I have ever seen!"
