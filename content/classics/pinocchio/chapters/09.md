# The Girl with Blue Hair

The night grew darker, and the wind kept pushing the puppet on his rope. He was almost gone when the little window opened again.

The girl with blue hair looked out. She had been standing there all the time, quiet as a candle, and she had seen everything. She clapped her hands three times. A big falcon flew down from the sky and landed on the window.

"What does my lady want?" asked the falcon, and he bowed his head. He was a polite bird.

"Do you see the puppet hanging from the oak?"

"I see him."

"Fly there at once. Cut the rope with your strong beak. Put him gently on the grass under the tree."

The falcon flew away. Two minutes later, he was back. "It is done," he said.

"Is the puppet alive or dead?"

"At first, he looked dead. But I put my beak close to his nose, and I felt a little breath. He is alive, but very, very ill."

The girl clapped her hands twice. A beautiful poodle came in, walking on his back legs like a man. He wore a gold coat, a lace collar and a hat with a feather. His long curly hair fell on his shoulders.

"Medoro," said the girl, "get the best carriage and go to the wood. Under the big oak, you will find a puppet on the ground. Lift him carefully, put him on the soft cushions and bring him here."

The poodle ran out. A moment later, a small carriage of glass and sugar came from the stable. A hundred white mice pulled it. Inside, there were soft cushions of cream and cake. When they brought Pinocchio home, the girl kissed his forehead and put him to bed. Then she sent for the three most famous doctors in the country.

They came one after the other: a Crow, an Owl and a Talking Cricket.

"First," said the girl, "I want to know if this poor puppet is alive or dead."

The Crow came forward. He felt the puppet's pulse, then his nose, then his toe. "I think that the puppet is dead," he said, in a serious voice. "But if he is not dead, then he is alive."

"I am sorry to disagree with my friend the Crow," said the Owl. "I think that the puppet is alive. But if he is not alive, then he is dead."

"And you?" the girl asked the Cricket.

"A wise doctor, when he does not know, says nothing," said the Cricket. "But I know this puppet. I have known him for a long time."

Pinocchio had been as still as a log. Now he shook so hard that the bed moved.

"This puppet," said the Cricket, "is a rascal and a disobedient boy. He will break his poor father's heart."

At that moment, a small cry of pain came from the bed. When they lifted the sheet, they saw Pinocchio crying.

"When a dead person cries," said the Crow, "it is a sign that he is getting better."

"I am sorry to disagree," said the Owl. "For me, it means that he is sorry to die."

The three doctors left. The girl took a glass of water and a white powder. "Drink this," she said. "In a few days, you will be well."

Pinocchio looked at the glass and made a face. "Is it sweet or bitter?" he asked.

"It is bitter. But it will make you well."

"If it is bitter, I do not want it."

"Drink it."

"I only like sweet things."

"Drink it, and I will give you a lump of sugar to take away the taste."

"Where is the sugar?"

"Here," said the girl. She took a lump from a golden bowl.

"Give me the sugar first. Then I will drink the bitter water."

"Do you promise?"

"Yes."

She gave him the sugar. He ate it in a second and licked his lips. "I wish that sugar was a medicine," he said. "I would take it every day."

"Now keep your promise and drink."

Pinocchio smelled the glass. He put it to his lips, and then he took it away. "It is too bitter. I cannot."

"You have not even tasted it."

"I can imagine it. Give me one more lump of sugar, and then I will drink."

The girl was losing patience, but she was kind. She gave him a second lump. When he ate it, she held out the glass again.

"I cannot," said the puppet.

At that moment, the door opened, and four black rabbits came in. They carried a small black coffin on their shoulders.

"What do you want from me?" cried Pinocchio, and he sat up in bed.

"We have come to take you away," said the biggest rabbit.

"Take me away? But I am not dead!"

"Not yet. But you will be soon. You refused to take the medicine that could make you well."

"Oh, my lady!" cried Pinocchio. "Give me the glass quickly! I do not want to die."

He took the glass in both hands and drank all of it in one gulp.

"Well," said the rabbits, "we came for nothing this time." They put the coffin on their shoulders again and left the room, grumbling.

A few minutes later, Pinocchio jumped out of bed, completely well. Wooden puppets are almost never ill. When they are ill, they get better very quickly.

The girl saw him running around the room, as happy as a young chicken. "So my medicine really made you well?"

"It saved my life!"

"Then why did I have to ask you so many times?"

"Because all children are the same," said Pinocchio. "We are more afraid of medicine than of illness."

"You should be ashamed. A good medicine at the right time can save you from a serious illness, and maybe from death."

"Next time, I will not need so much begging. I will remember the black rabbits and the coffin. I will drink at once."

"Now come here and tell me how you met the thieves."

Pinocchio told her everything. When he came to the gold coins, he stopped. "I lost them," he said.

It was a lie. He still had them in his pocket. As soon as he told the lie, his nose, which was already long, grew two inches longer.

"Where did you lose them?"

"In the wood nearby."

His nose grew two inches more.

"We will look for them in the wood," said the girl. "Everything lost there is found."

"Oh, now I remember," said Pinocchio. He was confused. "I did not lose them. I swallowed them when I drank the medicine."

At this third lie, his nose grew so long that he could not turn around in the room. If he turned right, he hit the bed. If he turned left, he hit the window. The girl looked at him and laughed.

"Why do you laugh?"

"Because of your lies."

"How do you know that I am lying?"

"Lies are easy to find, my dear boy. There are two kinds. There are lies with short legs, and lies with long noses. Yours have long noses."

Pinocchio was so ashamed that he tried to hide. But his nose was too long, and he could not even get through the door. He began to cry. He cried for half an hour, and the girl let him cry. She wanted to teach him a lesson. At last, she called a thousand woodpeckers. They flew in through the window and pecked at his nose, tap, tap, tap, until it was normal again.
