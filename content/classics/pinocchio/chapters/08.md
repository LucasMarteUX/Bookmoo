# Two Black Sacks and a Big Oak

"What a hard life a puppet has," Pinocchio thought, as he walked in the dark. "Everybody scolds us. Everybody gives us advice. If I listened to everyone, I would have three hundred fathers and three hundred Talking Crickets. I will not listen to that cricket. He said that thieves would meet me on the road. But I do not believe in thieves. Fathers and mothers invent them to scare children who want to go out at night. If I met them, I would not be afraid. I would say, 'Gentlemen, what do you want? Do not joke with me. Go away!' And they would run away like the wind."

He did not have time to think more. He heard a noise in the leaves.

He turned his head. In the dark, he saw two black shapes. They were wrapped from head to foot in coal sacks. They were running on tiptoe behind him, jumping like two ghosts.

"Here they are!" thought Pinocchio. He did not know where to hide his four gold coins. So he put them under his tongue. Then he began to run. But he had not taken a step when someone caught his arms. He heard two terrible voices. "Your money or your life!"

Pinocchio could not speak, because of the coins. He shook his head, and he made faces. He wanted to say that he was only a poor puppet and had no money at all.

"Stop playing games!" said the thieves. "Give us the money!"

Pinocchio's head and hands said, "No."

"Give us the money, or you are dead," said the taller thief.

"Dead," said the other.

"And after we kill you, we will kill your father too."

"Your father too!"

"No, no, no! Not my poor father!" screamed Pinocchio. But as he screamed, the coins rang in his mouth.

"Aha! You rascal! You hid the money under your tongue. Spit it out!"

But Pinocchio did not move his lips. He was very stubborn.

"Are you deaf? We will make you open your mouth."

One thief took Pinocchio by the nose, and the other took him by the chin. They pulled his face up and down. It was useless. His lips were like two pieces of wood nailed together.

At last, the shorter thief took out a long knife. He tried to open the puppet's mouth with it. But Pinocchio was as fast as lightning. He bit the thief's hand off and spat it out. And what did he see on the ground? It was not a hand. It was a cat's paw!

Pinocchio felt braver after his first victory. He pulled himself away from the thieves' claws. He jumped over a hedge and ran across the fields. The thieves ran after him like two dogs after a hare. The one without a paw ran on one leg. Nobody ever knew how he did it.

After fifteen miles, Pinocchio could not run any more. He climbed a tall pine tree and sat on a high branch. But the thieves climbed up after him. One of them fell, but he got up again, and he said, "Now we have you!"

Pinocchio jumped down and ran again. At last, he came to a dark forest, and in the forest, he saw a small white house. He knocked hard on the door. Nobody came.

The thieves were near. He knocked again, harder. "Help!" he cried.

At a little window, a girl appeared. She had blue hair, and her face was as white as wax. Her eyes were closed, and her hands were crossed on her chest. Without moving her lips, she spoke in a faint voice, as if from another world.

"Nobody lives in this house. We are all dead."

"At least open the door for me!" cried Pinocchio.

"I am dead too."

"Dead? Then what are you doing at the window?"

"I am waiting for the coffin to take me away."

After these words, the girl disappeared, and the window closed without a sound.

"Oh, beautiful girl with blue hair," shouted Pinocchio, "please open the door! Have pity on a poor boy! Two thieves are chasing..."

He did not finish. He felt two arms around his neck, and he heard the same two terrible voices. "Now you cannot escape!"

Pinocchio saw death in front of him. He shook so much that his wooden legs made a noise, and the four gold coins rang in his mouth.

"Well," said the thieves, "will you open your mouth, or not? You do not answer? Wait. This time, we will make you open it."

They took out two long, sharp knives, and they hit the puppet twice in the back. But Pinocchio was made of very hard wood, and the knives broke into a thousand pieces. The thieves looked at the handles in their hands. Then one of them said, "I know what to do. We will hang him."

They tied his hands behind his back and put a rope around his neck. They threw the end of the rope over the branch of a big oak tree. They pulled him up high. Then they sat on the grass to wait.

After three hours, Pinocchio's eyes were still open, and his mouth was still shut. He was kicking harder than before.

At last, the thieves lost patience. They said, laughing, "Goodbye until tomorrow. When we come back, we hope that you will be polite enough to be dead, with your mouth wide open." And they went away.

A cold north wind began to blow. It pushed the poor puppet from side to side, like the clapper of a bell. The rope was tight around his neck. He could not breathe. His eyes began to close. He still hoped that somebody would come. But nobody came. He thought of his poor father, and in a weak voice, he said, "Oh, Father, if you were here!"

He could not say more. He closed his eyes, opened his mouth, stretched out his legs and hung there, stiff and still.
