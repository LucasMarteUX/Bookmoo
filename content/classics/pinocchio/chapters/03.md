# The Cricket on the Wall

Pinocchio turned around. A big cricket was slowly walking up the wall.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"I am the Talking Cricket," said the insect. "I have lived in this room for more than a hundred years."

"Well, now this room is mine," said Pinocchio. "Go away, please, and do not come back."

"I will go," said the Cricket, "but first I want to tell you something important. Children who do not listen to their parents are never happy for long. One day, they are very sorry."

"Sing as much as you like," said Pinocchio. "I am leaving tomorrow morning. If I stay here, they will send me to school. I hate school! It is much better to run after butterflies, to climb trees and to look for birds' nests."

"Poor little fool," said the Cricket. "If you go on like that, you will grow up to be a donkey."

"Be quiet, you ugly bug!"

But the Cricket was an old animal, and he was very patient. "If you do not want to go to school," he said, "why do you not learn a trade? A good trade gives you bread."

"There is only one trade that I like," said Pinocchio. He was beginning to lose his patience. "I like to eat, to drink, to sleep and to play. I want to do that from morning till night."

"People who choose that trade," said the Cricket, calmly, "always finish in hospital or in prison."

"Be careful, Cricket! If I get angry, you will be sorry!"

"Poor Pinocchio. I feel very sorry for you."

"Why?"

"Because you are a puppet. And because you have a wooden head."

When he heard these words, Pinocchio jumped up in a rage. He took a wooden hammer from the bench and threw it at the Cricket.

Maybe he did not want to hit him. But the hammer flew straight, and it hit the Cricket on the head. The poor animal said "cri" once, and he fell down. He stayed there, flat against the wall, and he did not move again.

It was night now, and Pinocchio began to feel hungry. He had not eaten anything all day. His stomach made small, angry noises. He looked around the room for food. There was nothing. There was no bread, there was no fruit, and there was not even a bone. He opened every cupboard, and he looked in every box. Nothing.

His hunger became bigger and bigger. He yawned so much that his mouth was like a wide hole. At last, he began to cry.

"The Cricket was right," he said. "I was wrong to run away. If Father were here, I would not be so hungry. Hunger is a terrible illness!"

Suddenly, in a pile of rubbish, he saw something round and white. It looked like an egg. In one jump, he was there. It really was an egg!

He could not believe his luck. He turned it over and over in his hands. He kissed it. "How will I cook you?" he said. "Shall I make an omelet? Or shall I boil you? I think that it is faster to cook you in a pan."

He put a little pan on a dish of hot coals. There was no butter and no oil in the house, so he poured in some water. When the water began to bubble, he broke the egg over the pan.

But no yellow and white came out. A small, living chick came out instead. It shook its feathers and made a polite little bow.

"Thank you very much, Mr. Pinocchio," said the chick. "You saved me the trouble of breaking the shell. Goodbye, and say hello to your family!" It opened its wings and flew out of the window. It was soon gone.

Pinocchio stood there with the empty shell in his hand. His eyes were wide, and his mouth was open. When he understood what had happened, he began to cry. He cried, he screamed and he stamped on the floor. "The Cricket was right! If I had not run away, I would not be hungry now!"

His stomach was louder than ever. He did not know how to make it quiet. At last, he decided to go to the village. Maybe a kind person there would give him a piece of bread.
