# Five Gold Coins

The giant, whose name was Fire-Eater, was not as bad as he looked. His heart was soft, and whenever he felt pity, he sneezed. It was a habit from childhood. The puppets knew this very well. They always cried when they wanted something from him.

"Stop crying," he said, and he rubbed his nose. "You make me sneeze. All right. I will not burn Harlequin tonight. I will find another way to cook my sheep. But I need some wood. Take this one!" He picked up a puppet with a red nose and put him on the fire. The sheep was soon ready. Pinocchio watched, and he felt sad for the poor puppet. But he was glad that Harlequin was safe.

After supper, Fire-Eater called Pinocchio to the table. "Tell me about your family," he said. "Who is your father?"

Pinocchio told him about Geppetto. He told him about the burned feet, the new feet, the paper clothes, the coat and the alphabet book.

"What a poor man," said Fire-Eater. His nose began to itch again. "I wanted to burn you. But now I see that you are a good son. Tell me, where is your book?"

"I sold it to buy a ticket," said Pinocchio. He looked at the floor. "I am sorry."

Fire-Eater took five shining gold coins from his pocket. They were so bright that they seemed to light the room. "Take these," he said. "Give them to your father. Tell him that I send them. He must buy a warm coat. You saved Harlequin and made my puppets happy. Thank you."

Pinocchio could not speak. He thanked the big man a hundred times. He kissed his hands and the hands of every puppet. He said goodbye to Harlequin with tears in his eyes. Then he left the theater with the five gold coins in his pocket.

It was early morning. The road was empty, and the sun was rising. The birds sang in the trees. Pinocchio felt rich and happy. "I will give three coins to my father," he said to himself. "With the other two, I will buy a new jacket, shoes and a real alphabet book. I will be the best student in the school!"

He had not walked half a mile when he met two strange travelers. One was a fox who walked on three legs, and one was a cat who could not see. They went along the road together, and they helped each other like good friends. The fox leaned on the cat, and the cat followed the fox.

"Good morning, Pinocchio," said the fox, in a sweet voice.

"How do you know my name?"

"I know your father well. I saw him yesterday in front of his house. He was only in his shirt, and he was shaking with cold."

"Poor Father," said Pinocchio. "But he will not be cold any more. I have become rich."

"You? Rich?" said the fox. He began to laugh. The cat laughed too, but she cleaned her whiskers to hide it.

"I do not think it is funny," said Pinocchio. "Look!" He took out the coins and shook them. They made a lovely sound. The fox put out his bad paw, as if by accident. The cat opened her blind eyes wide, and they shone like two green lamps. But Pinocchio did not see this.

"And what will you do with so much money?" asked the fox.

"First, I will buy my father a coat of gold and silver, with diamond buttons. Then I will buy an alphabet book for myself."

"For yourself?"

"Yes, because I want to study and be a good boy."

"Look at me," said the fox. "I studied too much, and I lost my leg."

"And I studied too much, and I lost my eyes," said the cat.

A white blackbird sat on a bush near the road. "Pinocchio," it said, "do not listen to bad friends. If you do, you will be sorry."

But the cat jumped. With one bite, she ate the poor blackbird, feathers and all. Pinocchio was a little afraid. But he did not turn back.

"Tell me," said the fox in his sweetest voice. "Do you want to make your five gold coins into two thousand?"

"Two thousand? How?"

"It is easy. Do not go home. Come with us."

"Where?"

"To the Land of the Owls."

Pinocchio thought for a moment. He remembered the Cricket, and he remembered his father. But he also thought about two thousand gold coins. Even a wise boy can be silly when he has a dream.

"All right," he said. "I will go with you."
