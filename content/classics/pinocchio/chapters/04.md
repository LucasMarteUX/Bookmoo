# Burned Feet, Pears and a Coat

It was a terrible night. The sky was black, and the thunder was loud. Lightning flashed again and again. Pinocchio was afraid of thunder. But his hunger was stronger than his fear. He ran to the village as fast as he could.

The village was dark and quiet. All the doors were closed, and all the shops were shut. There was nobody in the streets, not even a dog. It looked like a village of ghosts.

Pinocchio was tired, wet and desperate. He ran to the first house and rang the bell again and again. After a while, an old man opened a window and looked out. He wore a nightcap, and he was very angry.

"What do you want at this hour?" he shouted.

"Please, can you give me a little bread?" said Pinocchio.

"Wait there. I will be back," said the old man. He thought that Pinocchio was one of those bad boys who ring doorbells at night for fun.

A minute later, the window opened again. "Stand under the window," said the old man, "and hold out your cap."

Pinocchio had no cap. But he stood under the window anyway. At once, a bucket of cold water fell on his head. He was wet from head to foot, like a flower that someone forgot in the rain.

He went home tired and hungry and wet. He did not have the strength to stand, so he sat on a small stool. He put his feet on the pan of hot coals to dry them. And because he was so tired, he fell asleep.

While he was sleeping, his wooden feet began to burn. Slowly, they became black. Then they became ash. And Pinocchio slept and snored, as if the feet belonged to someone else.

In the morning, somebody knocked at the door.

"Who is it?" asked Pinocchio, with a yawn.

"It is I," said a voice.

It was Geppetto. The police had let him out of prison that morning.

Pinocchio did not look at his feet. He jumped off the stool to open the door. But after two or three steps, he fell on the floor with a big noise, like a bag of spoons.

"Open the door!" shouted Geppetto from the street.

"I cannot, Father," said Pinocchio. He was crying and rolling on the floor. "Somebody ate my feet!"

"Who ate them?"

"The cat," said Pinocchio, because he saw the cat in the corner. She was playing with the wood shavings.

"Open the door, or I will beat you!"

"I cannot stand up! Oh, poor me! I will walk on my knees for the rest of my life!"

Geppetto thought that this was another trick. He climbed up the wall and went in through the window. At first, he was angry. But when he saw Pinocchio on the floor with no feet, he felt only pity. He took him in his arms and kissed him. He said a thousand loving words, and tears ran down his face.

"My little Pinocchio, how did you burn your feet?"

"I do not know, Father. It was a terrible night. There was thunder and lightning, and I was so hungry. I killed the Talking Cricket. Then I found an egg, and a chicken came out and said goodbye. Then I went out, and a man poured water on my head. Then I put my feet on the coals, and now they are burned. And I am still hungry! Hi, hi, hi!"

He cried so loudly that people heard him from far away.

Geppetto understood only one thing: the boy was hungry. He took three pears out of his pocket. "These were my breakfast," he said. "But you can have them. Eat them, and I hope that you feel better."

"Please peel them for me."

"Peel them?" said Geppetto, surprised. "I never knew that you were so fussy. That is not good. In this life, we must learn to eat everything. We never know what will happen."

"You are right," said Pinocchio. "But I never eat a pear with the skin."

So kind Geppetto peeled the three pears. He put the skins on the corner of the table. Pinocchio ate the first pear in two bites. He was going to throw away the middle part, but Geppetto held his arm.

"Do not throw it away. In this world, everything is useful."

"I will never eat that!" said Pinocchio.

"Who knows?" said Geppetto, calmly.

When Pinocchio finished the three pears, he yawned and began to cry again. "I am still hungry!"

"But I have nothing more. Only the skins and the middles."

"Then I will eat a skin," said Pinocchio. He made a face. But he ate one skin, then another. After that, he ate all the middles, too. When he finished, he touched his stomach, and he smiled. "Now I feel better!"

"You see?" said Geppetto. "We must never be too fussy."

Then Geppetto took his tools. He worked for two hours, and he made two new feet. They were small and neat, and they looked as if an artist had made them. He fixed them to Pinocchio's legs, while the puppet pretended to be asleep. As soon as Pinocchio felt his new feet, he jumped from the table and began to dance around the room. He laughed and jumped for joy.

"To thank you for all this," he said, "I will go to school today."

"That is a good boy."

"But I need clothes."

Geppetto had no money. He made a little suit of flowered paper, a pair of shoes from the bark of a tree and a small cap from the soft part of bread. Pinocchio ran to look at himself in a bowl of water. "I look like a real gentleman!" he said happily.

"Yes," said Geppetto. "But remember: good clothes do not make a gentleman. A clean heart makes a gentleman."

"But I need one more thing to go to school," said Pinocchio. "I need an alphabet book."

"You are right. But we have no money."

"Neither do I," said Pinocchio, and he became sad. Even a puppet understands poverty.

Geppetto suddenly stood up. He took his old coat, which was full of holes and patches, and he ran out of the house. When he came back, he had an alphabet book in his hand. But he did not have his coat. The poor man was only in his shirt, and it was snowing outside.

"Where is your coat, Father?"

"I sold it."

"Why?"

"Because it was too warm."

Pinocchio understood at once. He threw his arms around the old man's neck and kissed his face again and again.
