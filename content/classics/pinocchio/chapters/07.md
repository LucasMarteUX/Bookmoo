# The Inn of the Red Lobster

They walked all day. By the evening, they were so tired that they could hardly stand. At last, they came to a little inn called the Red Lobster.

"Let us stop here," said the fox. "We will eat something and sleep for a few hours. At midnight, we will go on. Tomorrow, at sunrise, we will be at the Field of Miracles."

They went in and sat down at a table. None of them was very hungry. The cat said that she felt ill, and she ate only thirty-five fish with tomato sauce and four plates of tripe. The fox said that he must be careful with his health. He ate one small hare and a few chickens. Nobody could say how they ate so much. Pinocchio was so sleepy that he could not think. He ate only a few nuts and a piece of bread, and he left most of them on his plate. His head was full of the Field of Miracles. He was already dreaming of gold coins.

When they finished eating, the fox said to the innkeeper, "Please give us two good rooms. One room for Mr. Pinocchio, and one for me and my friend. We will leave at midnight. Please wake us then."

"Yes, sir," said the innkeeper. He smiled and winked at the fox and the cat. He knew them well. They were old friends.

Pinocchio went to bed and fell asleep at once. He had a beautiful dream. He was in a field full of trees. The trees were covered with gold coins. When the wind blew, the coins made a sound like little bells: zin, zin, zin. They seemed to say, "Come and take us!" Pinocchio put out his hand to take one.

But three loud knocks woke him up. It was the innkeeper. "It is midnight," he said.

"Are my friends ready?"

"They left two hours ago."

"Why so early?"

"The cat got a message. Her little son was very ill, and she could not wait. She asked me to say goodbye. She hopes to see you again soon."

"Did they pay for the supper?"

"No, sir. They are too polite to do that to a gentleman like you."

"What a pity," said Pinocchio. "I would not have minded." He paid for the supper with one gold coin. Then he asked, "Is the road to the Field of Miracles safe?"

"Very safe. There is only one problem. There may be some bad people in the wood. But if you keep your eyes open, you will have nothing to fear."

So Pinocchio set out into the night. It was so dark that he could not see his own hand. The bushes moved, and the leaves whispered. A bat flew into his nose and gave him such a fright that he cried, "Who is there?" And the hills answered, "Who is there? Who is there?"

As he walked, he saw a little insect on a tree trunk. It shone with a pale light, like a candle behind glass.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"I am the ghost of the Talking Cricket," said the insect, in a faint, faraway voice. "Listen to me, Pinocchio. Go back home. Take your four gold coins to your poor father. He is crying because you did not come back."

"Tomorrow my father will be a rich man. These four coins will become two thousand."

"Do not trust people who promise you riches in one night, my boy. They are usually fools or cheats. Go back."

"I do not want to go back."

"The night is dark."

"I do not care."

"The road is dangerous."

"I do not care."

"Remember: boys who always do what they want are sorry sooner or later."

"The same old story. Good night, Cricket."

"Good night, Pinocchio. May the sky protect you from thieves."

The little light went out, as if someone blew on a candle. The road was darker than before.
