# The Puppet Theater

Pinocchio put the alphabet book under his arm and walked to school. The snow had stopped. The sun was shining on the white roofs. As he walked, he made big plans.

"Today I will learn to read," he said to himself. "Tomorrow I will learn to write. The day after tomorrow, I will learn arithmetic. Then I will be so clever that I will earn a lot of money. With my first money, I will buy my father a new coat. A cloth coat? No, a coat of gold and silver, with diamond buttons! He sold his coat for me. He is so cold! He deserves the best coat in the world."

He was so happy with these ideas that his eyes filled with tears.

Suddenly, he heard music from far away: a flute and a drum. Pi-pi-pi, zum, zum, zum. It came from the end of the road, near the sea.

"What is that music?" he said. "What a pity that I have to go to school. If I did not, I would go and see."

He stopped in the middle of the road. He did not know what to do. The music was so merry. It seemed to say, "Come, come, come!" At last, he decided. "Today, I will go and hear the flute. Tomorrow, I will go to school. There is always time for school."

And he began to run.

The nearer he came, the louder the music was. At last, he arrived in a little square full of people. They were all looking at a big tent of colored cloth. On the front, there was a sign in big red letters: THE GREAT PUPPET THEATER.

"How much is a ticket?" Pinocchio asked a boy.

"Four pennies."

Pinocchio had no money. "Can you lend me four pennies until tomorrow?" he asked, with a big smile.

"I would like to," said the boy. "But today I cannot."

"I will sell you my paper jacket for four pennies."

"A paper jacket? If it rains, I cannot take it off."

"Do you want my shoes?"

"They are only good for the fire."

"What about my bread cap?"

"The mice would eat it from my head."

Pinocchio did not know what to do. He had only one thing left, his alphabet book. But he did not want to sell it. His father had paid for it with his coat. Then he thought of the music. He looked at the people going into the tent. He felt a big pain in his heart.

"Does anyone want to buy an alphabet book?" he said, very quietly.

A man in an old hat stood near him. "I will buy it," he said. "It is a nice new book. Four pennies."

In a few minutes, Pinocchio was inside the theater. He felt small and proud at the same time. He did not think about his father, and he did not think about the coat.

The show had already started. On the stage, two puppets, Harlequin and Punch, were arguing and shouting. The people were laughing at their jokes. Some of them held their sides.

Suddenly, Harlequin stopped. He looked at the people, and he pointed at the back of the room. "I am not dreaming, am I? That is Pinocchio!"

"It is Pinocchio!" cried Punch.

"It is Pinocchio!" cried all the puppets. They ran from every side of the stage. "It is our brother Pinocchio! Hurray!"

"Come up here, Pinocchio!" called Harlequin. "Come to your wooden brothers!"

Pinocchio could not say no to such a warm welcome. He jumped from the back of the room to the front seats. Then he jumped on the head of the man with the drum. Then he jumped onto the stage. The puppets hugged him and kissed him and pinched him in a friendly way.

It was a nice sight. But the people in the seats were not happy. They had paid for a play, not for a family party. "We want the show!" they shouted. "Go on with the show!"

The puppets did not listen. They put Pinocchio on their shoulders and carried him around the stage.

At that moment, the owner of the theater walked in. He was so big and so ugly that people were afraid just to look at him. His beard was black as ink and so long that it touched the floor. His mouth was as wide as a door, and his eyes were like two red lamps. In his hand, he had a big whip made of snakes and fox tails.

Everything became quiet. Nobody breathed. Even the flies stopped flying. All the puppets shook like leaves in the wind.

"Why did you come to my theater and spoil my show?" said the giant to Pinocchio. His voice was like a bear with a cold.

"It was not my fault, sir," said Pinocchio.

"Be quiet. We will talk about it tonight."

When the show ended, the giant went into his kitchen. He was cooking a big fat sheep for his supper, but he did not have enough wood for the fire. "Bring me Harlequin and Pinocchio!" he shouted. "They are made of dry wood. They will burn very well."

Harlequin and Pinocchio were so afraid that they could not move. They fell on the floor and shook. But Pinocchio crawled to the giant's feet. He began to cry.

"Please, sir, be kind!"

"I am not a sir here."

"Please, Mister, be kind!"

"I am not a mister here."

"Please, Your Excellency!"

When he heard this word, the giant stopped. His face became gentle. "Well," he said. "What do you want?"

"Please do not burn Harlequin. I never had a friend before. If you must burn someone, burn me. It is better. Oh, my poor father!"

His words made all the puppets cry. Even the giant felt a strange feeling in his nose. It happened to him whenever he felt pity. He tried to stop it, but he could not. He sneezed loudly.

"Atchoo!"

"God bless you," said Pinocchio.
