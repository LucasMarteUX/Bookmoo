# Lapland and Finland

In the morning, Gerda told the little robber girl everything that the pigeons had said. The girl looked serious, but she nodded her head.

"It does not matter," she said. "Do you know where Lapland is?" she asked the reindeer.

"Who could know it better than I?" said the animal, and his eyes shone. "I was born and raised there. I ran about on the snow fields."

"Listen," said the robber girl to Gerda. "All our men are away. My mother is still here, and she will stay. But later in the morning, she drinks from the big bottle, and then she sleeps a little. Then I will do something for you."

She jumped out of bed. She flew at her mother's neck, pulled her moustache, and said, "Good morning, my own sweet nanny goat." Her mother gave her a push on the nose, so that it became red and blue. But it was all done with love.

When the mother had drunk from her bottle and was sleeping, the robber girl went to the reindeer.

"I would like to tickle you a few more times with my sharp knife," she said. "You are so funny when I do it. But never mind. I will untie your rope and help you out of the door. You must run to Lapland as fast as you can. And you must take this little girl to the palace of the Snow Queen, where her playmate is. You heard what she told me. She talked loudly enough, and you were listening."

The reindeer jumped for joy. The robber girl lifted little Gerda on to his back. She was careful to tie her fast, and she even gave her a little cushion to sit on.

"Here are your fur boots," she said. "It will be cold. But I will keep the muff. It is much too pretty. Still, you shall not freeze. Here are my mother's big gloves. They will reach up to your elbows. Put them on. Now your hands look like my ugly mother's."

Gerda wept for joy.

"I cannot bear to see you crying," said the robber girl. "You should look glad now. Here are two loaves and a ham, so that you will not starve."

She tied them on the reindeer's back. She opened the door, called in all the big dogs, and cut the rope with her knife. She said to the reindeer, "Now run, but take good care of the little girl."

Gerda stretched out her hands in the big gloves toward the robber girl, and she said goodbye. The reindeer flew over stumps and stones, through the great forest, over marshes and over the steppe, as fast as he could. The wolves howled, and the ravens screamed. "Sh, sh!" it sounded in the sky, as if it were sneezing red.

"Those are my old northern lights," said the reindeer. "See how they shine!" He ran faster and faster, day and night. The loaves were eaten, and the ham too. And then they were in Lapland.

They stopped at a tiny house. It was very poor. The roof reached down to the ground, and the door was so low that the family had to creep on their stomachs to go in and out. Nobody was at home except an old Lapland woman, who was cooking fish over an oil lamp. The reindeer told her all about Gerda's story, but first his own, because it seemed to him much more important. Gerda was so cold that she could not speak.

"Poor things," said the Lapland woman. "You have far to run yet. You must go hundreds of miles into Finland. The Snow Queen is staying there now, and she burns blue lights every night. I will write a few words on a piece of dried codfish, for I have no paper. You will take it to the Finland woman. She can tell you more than I can."

When Gerda was warm, and had eaten and drunk, the woman wrote a few words on the dried fish. She told Gerda to take care of it. She tied her again on the reindeer's back, and away he went. "Sh, sh!" it sounded in the air. The most beautiful blue northern lights burned all night long.

At last, they came to Finland, and they knocked at the chimney of the Finland woman, for she had no door. It was so hot inside that the Finland woman herself went almost naked. She was small and rather dirty. She at once loosened little Gerda's clothes and took off her gloves and boots. Otherwise, it would have been too hot for her. Then she put a piece of ice on the reindeer's head and read what was written on the dried fish. She read it three times. When she knew it by heart, she put the fish into the soup pot, because it could be eaten, and she never wasted anything.

The reindeer first told his own story, and then little Gerda's. The Finland woman blinked her wise eyes, but she said nothing.

"You are very clever," said the reindeer. "I know that you can tie all the winds of the world with a thread. If a sailor unties one knot, he gets a good wind. If he unties the second, it blows hard. If he unties the third and fourth, there is a storm that throws down the forests. Will you give the little girl a drink, that she may have the strength of twelve men, and overcome the Snow Queen?"

"The strength of twelve men!" said the Finland woman. "That would be very useful!" She went to a shelf and took down a big rolled-up skin. She unrolled it. Strange letters were written on it. The woman read them until the sweat ran down her forehead.

But the reindeer begged so hard for little Gerda, and Gerda looked at the Finland woman with such loving, tearful eyes, that the woman began to blink again. She drew the reindeer into a corner. She whispered to him, as she put new ice on his head.

"Little Kai is really with the Snow Queen," she said. "He finds everything there to his liking, and he thinks that it is the best place in the world. But that is because he has a splinter of glass in his heart, and a little piece in his eye. They must come out first, or he will never be a man again, and the Snow Queen will keep her power over him."

"But can you not give little Gerda something to take, to give her power over it all?"

"I can give her no greater power than she has already. Do you not see how great it is? Do you not see how people and animals must serve her? How she has come so far in the wide world, on her bare feet? She must not hear of her power from us. It is in her heart. It is because she is a sweet and innocent child. If she cannot get to the Snow Queen by herself, and take out the glass, we cannot help her. Two miles from here, the garden of the Snow Queen begins. You can carry the little girl there. Put her down by the big bush that stands in the snow, with red berries. Do not stay gossiping, but hurry back."

The Finland woman lifted Gerda on to the reindeer's back, and he ran off as fast as he could.
