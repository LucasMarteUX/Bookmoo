# The Little Robber Girl

The coach drove through a dark forest. It shone like a torch. It hurt the eyes of the robbers, and they could not bear it.

"It is gold! It is gold!" they cried. They rushed out, seized the horses, killed the little drivers and footmen, and pulled little Gerda out of the coach.

"She is fat and pretty. She has been fed on nuts," said an old robber woman, who had a long, rough beard, and eyebrows that hung down over her eyes. "She is as good as a fat little lamb. How good she will taste!" She drew out her shining knife. It glittered terribly.

"Ouch!" cried the woman at the same moment. Her own little daughter, who sat on her back, had bitten her ear. The girl was wild and bad, and the mother was very angry.

"You ugly girl!" said the mother. She had no time to kill Gerda.

"She shall play with me," said the little robber girl. "She shall give me her muff and her pretty dress, and sleep with me in my bed." And she bit her mother again, so that the woman jumped up and turned around. All the robbers laughed. "Look how she dances with her child!" they said.

"I will go in the coach," said the little robber girl. She always did what she wanted. She sat in the coach with Gerda, and they drove over stumps and stones, deep into the forest. The little robber girl was as tall as Gerda, but stronger, with broader shoulders and a dark skin. Her eyes were black and almost sad. She put her arm around Gerda's waist.

"They shall not kill you as long as I am not angry with you," she said. "You are a princess, I think."

"No," said Gerda. She told the robber girl all that had happened, and how much she loved little Kai.

The robber girl looked at her seriously. She nodded her head a little. "They shall not kill you, even if I am angry with you," she said. "Then I will do it myself."

She dried Gerda's eyes. Then she put both her hands in the pretty muff, which was so soft and warm.

The coach stopped. They were in the middle of the courtyard of a robber's castle. It was cracked from the top to the bottom. Ravens and crows flew out of the open holes. Big bulldogs, each of which looked as if he could swallow a man, jumped high up, but they did not bark. That was forbidden.

In the big old smoky hall, a great fire burned in the middle of the stone floor. The smoke went up under the ceiling and had to find its own way out. A big pot of soup was boiling, and hares and rabbits were being roasted on the fire.

"You shall sleep with me tonight, with all my animals," said the robber girl. They had something to eat and drink. Then they went to a corner, where straw and carpets lay. Above them, on rods and sticks, sat nearly a hundred pigeons. They all seemed to be asleep, but they moved a little when the little robber girl came.

"They are all mine," she said. She took hold of one by the feet and shook it so that it beat its wings. "Kiss it," she cried, and she hit Gerda in the face with it. "There sit the rascals of the wood," she went on, pointing to some sticks that were nailed over a hole high in the wall. "Those are wood pigeons. They would fly away at once, if they were not locked up. And here is my old darling, Bae." She pulled a reindeer by the horn. It had a bright copper ring around its neck, and it was tied up. "We have to keep him tight, too, or he will jump away. Every evening, I tickle his neck with my sharp knife. He is so afraid of it."

The little girl pulled out a long knife from a crack in the wall. She let it glide over the reindeer's neck. The poor animal kicked. The robber girl laughed, and she pulled Gerda into bed with her.

"Do you keep the knife while you sleep?" asked Gerda. She looked at it a little fearfully.

"I always sleep with a knife," said the little robber girl. "You never know what may happen. But tell me again what you told me before about little Kai, and why you have gone out into the wide world."

Gerda told her story again from the beginning. The wood pigeons cooed in their cage. The others slept. The little robber girl put her arm around Gerda's neck. She held the knife in the other hand, and she snored. But Gerda could not close her eyes. She did not know if she would live or die.

Then the wood pigeons said, "Coo, coo! We have seen little Kai. A white hen carried his sled. He sat in the Snow Queen's sleigh, which flew over the forest when we lay in our nest. She blew on us young ones, and all died except us two. Coo, coo!"

"What are you saying up there?" cried Gerda. "Where did the Snow Queen go? Do you know anything?"

"She was probably going to Lapland, where there is always snow and ice. Ask the reindeer who is tied up there."

"There is ice and snow," said the reindeer. "It is a lovely place. One can run free in the great sparkling valleys. There the Snow Queen has her summer tent. But her strong castle is near the North Pole, on the island called Spitzbergen."

"Oh, Kai, little Kai!" sighed Gerda.

"Lie still," said the robber girl, "or I will put my knife in your stomach."
