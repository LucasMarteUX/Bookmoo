# The Palace of Ice

"Oh, I have not got my boots! I have not got my gloves!" cried little Gerda. She felt it in the sharp frost. But the reindeer did not dare to stop. He ran on until he came to the big bush with the red berries. There he put Gerda down and kissed her mouth. Big tears ran down his cheeks. Then he ran back as fast as he could.

There stood poor Gerda. She had no shoes and no gloves, in the middle of the dreadfully cold land of Finland.

She ran forward as fast as she could. A whole army of snowflakes came toward her. They did not fall from the sky, which was clear and shone with the northern lights. The snowflakes ran along the ground, and the nearer they came, the bigger they grew. Gerda remembered how big and strange they had looked through the magnifying glass. But these were much bigger and more terrible. They were alive. They were the guards of the Snow Queen. They had the strangest shapes. Some looked like big ugly hedgehogs. Others were like knots of snakes that stretched out their heads. Others were like fat little bears with bristling fur. All were shining white. All were living snowflakes.

Then little Gerda said her Lord's Prayer. The cold was so great that she could see her own breath, which rose like smoke from her mouth. The breath grew thicker and thicker. It took the shape of little bright angels, who grew bigger and bigger as they touched the ground. All of them had helmets on their heads, and spears and shields in their hands. Their number grew. When Gerda had finished her prayer, she was surrounded by a whole legion. They struck the terrible snowflakes with their spears, so that the flakes flew into a hundred pieces. Little Gerda went on, safe and cheerful. The angels stroked her hands and feet, and she felt the cold less.

She hurried on to the Snow Queen's palace.

Now we must see what Kai was doing. He was not thinking of Gerda. He did not dream that she was standing outside the palace.

The walls of the palace were made of drifting snow. The windows and doors were made of cutting winds. There were more than a hundred halls, all as the snow had blown them. The largest was many miles long. They were all lit by the strong northern lights. They were so big and empty, so icy cold and glittering. There was never any fun there, not even a little bear party, where the storm could have been the music, and the polar bears could have walked on their hind legs and shown their fine manners. Never a little game of cards, with a quarrel and a fight. Never a little coffee party for the white fox ladies. Empty, big, and cold were the halls of the Snow Queen. The northern lights flashed so clearly that one could tell when they were at their highest and when they were at their lowest. In the middle of this empty, endless hall of snow, there was a frozen lake. It had broken into a thousand pieces, but each piece was so exactly like the others that it was a wonderful work of art. In the middle of it sat the Snow Queen when she was at home. Then she said that she sat in the Mirror of Understanding, and that this was the only one, and the best thing in the world.

Little Kai was quite blue with cold. He was nearly black. But he did not feel it, because the Snow Queen had kissed away the shivering feeling. His heart was like a lump of ice. He was dragging some flat, pointed pieces of ice to and fro, and putting them together in every possible way. He wanted to make something out of them. It was like when we make pictures with little pieces of wood, which we call the Chinese puzzle. Kai made figures, too, and the most beautiful ones. It was the ice puzzle of reason. In his eyes, the figures were very remarkable and of the highest importance. That was because of the splinter of glass in his eye. He made figures that formed a written word. But he could never make the word that he really wanted. That word was "Eternity." The Snow Queen had said to him, "If you can make that figure, you shall be your own master. I will give you the whole world and a new pair of skates." But he could not do it.

"Now I am going to the warm countries," said the Snow Queen. "I want to look into the black pots." She meant the volcanoes Etna and Vesuvius. "I will make them a little white. That is good for the lemons and the grapes." And away she flew. Kai sat quite alone in the great empty hall of ice. He looked at his pieces of ice, and he thought and thought. He was so still and stiff that one might think that he was frozen.

At that moment, little Gerda stepped through the great door of the palace. Cutting winds were blowing there. But she said her evening prayer, and the winds lay down, as if they were going to sleep. She stepped into the great empty hall.

She saw Kai. She knew him at once. She flew to him, and she put her arms around his neck. She held him tight and cried, "Kai! Dear little Kai! At last, I have found you!"

But he sat still, stiff and cold.

Then Gerda wept hot tears. They fell on his chest, and they went to his heart. They melted the lump of ice, and they washed away the little piece of glass. He looked at her. She sang her old song:

"Roses grow in the valley,
There we shall see the Christ child."

Then Kai burst into tears. He cried so hard that the splinter of glass was washed out of his eye. He knew her, and he cried out with joy: "Gerda! Dear little Gerda! Where have you been so long? And where have I been?" He looked around him. "How cold it is here! How big and empty!" He clung to Gerda. She laughed and wept for joy.

It was such a happy moment that even the pieces of ice danced around them. When they were tired and lay down, they formed the very letters that the Snow Queen had told Kai to find. If he could make that word, he would be his own master, and she would give him the whole world and a new pair of skates. The word was Eternity.
