# The Prince and the Princess

"The newspapers came out the next day with a border of hearts," said the crow, "and the princess's initials. They said that every young man with a good appearance was free to come to the palace and speak with the princess. The one who spoke so that she could hear that he felt at home, and who spoke best, she would take for her husband.

"Yes, yes," said the crow, "you can believe me. It is as true as I sit here. People came running in great crowds. There was a great noise and a great push, but nobody was lucky, neither on the first nor the second day. They could all talk well, when they were in the street. But when they came inside the palace gates, and saw the guards in silver, and the footmen in gold on the stairs, and the great lighted rooms, they were confused. And when they stood in front of the throne where the princess sat, they could say nothing except the last word that she had said. And she did not care about hearing that again. It was as if they had all taken a sleeping drink, and fallen asleep. They did not wake up again until they were out in the street. Then they could talk. There was a long line of them from the town gates to the palace. I went myself to look at it," said the crow. "They were hungry and thirsty, but at the palace, they did not get even a glass of lukewarm water. Some of the clever ones had brought sandwiches. But they did not share them with their neighbors. They thought, 'Let him look hungry. Then the princess will not take him.'"

"But Kai, little Kai?" asked Gerda. "When did he come? Was he among the crowd?"

"Wait, wait! We are coming to him. On the third day, a little person came marching right up to the palace. He had no horse or carriage. His eyes shone like yours. He had beautiful long hair, but his clothes were poor."

"That was Kai!" said Gerda, with joy. "Oh, then I have found him!" She clapped her hands.

"He had a little knapsack on his back," said the crow.

"No, that must have been his sled," said Gerda. "He went away with his sled."

"It may be," said the crow. "I did not look so closely. But I know from my tame sweetheart that when he came to the palace gate and saw the guards in silver, and the footmen on the stairs, he was not at all afraid. He nodded and said to them, 'It must be tiresome to stand on the stairs. I will rather go in.' The rooms shone with lamps. The great officials were walking about in their best shoes, which made no noise, and they carried golden dishes. It was solemn. His boots creaked so loudly, but he was not afraid."

"That must be Kai," said Gerda. "I know that he had new boots. I heard them creak in my grandmother's room."

"Yes, they did creak," said the crow. "And he went boldly up to the princess. She sat on a pearl as big as a spinning wheel. All the ladies of the court, with their maids and the maids of their maids, and all the gentlemen with their servants and the servants of their servants, who also had a boy, stood around. The nearer they stood to the door, the prouder they looked. The boy of the servant's servant, who always goes in slippers, can hardly be looked at, he stands so proudly in the doorway."

"That must be terrible," said little Gerda. "And yet Kai won the princess?"

"If I were not a crow, I would have taken her myself, even though I am engaged. They say that he spoke as well as I do when I speak crow language. That is what my tame sweetheart says. He was happy and pleasant. He had not come to woo the princess, but only to hear her wisdom. And he liked it, and she liked him."

"Yes, that was certainly Kai," said Gerda. "He was so clever. He could do arithmetic in his head, even with fractions. Oh, will you not take me to the palace?"

"That is easily said," answered the crow. "But how shall we do it? I will speak to my tame sweetheart about it. She can advise us. But I must tell you that a little girl like you will never be allowed to come in the front way."

"Oh, yes, I will," said Gerda. "When Kai hears that I am here, he will come out at once to fetch me."

"Wait for me by the gate," said the crow. He shook his head and flew away.

It was late in the evening when the crow came back. "Caw, caw!" he said. "My sweetheart sends you many greetings. And here is a little bread for you. She took it from the kitchen. There is plenty there, and you must be hungry. It is not possible for you to come into the palace, because you have bare feet. The guards in silver and the footmen in gold would not allow it. But do not cry. You shall go in. My sweetheart knows a little back staircase that leads up to the bedroom, and she knows where to find the key."

So they went into the garden, into the big avenue where one leaf fell after another. When the lights in the palace had been put out, one after another, the crow led little Gerda to a back door, which stood ajar.

Oh, how Gerda's heart beat with fear and longing! It was as if she was going to do something wrong. And yet she only wanted to know if it was little Kai. Yes, it must be him. She thought so clearly of his clever eyes and his long hair. She could see how he smiled, as he did when they sat under the roses at home. He would surely be glad to see her, and to hear what a long way she had come for his sake. He would be so happy.

Now they were on the stairs. A little lamp was burning on a shelf. In the middle of the floor stood the tame crow, turning her head on every side and looking at Gerda, who made a curtsy, as her grandmother had taught her.

"My intended has told me so many good things about you, my little lady," said the tame crow. "Your story is also very touching. If you will take the lamp, I will go before. We will go straight on. We shall not meet anyone."

"I think that someone is coming behind us," said Gerda. Something rushed past her. It was like shadows on the wall: horses with flying manes and thin legs, huntsmen, ladies and gentlemen on horseback.

"They are only dreams," said the tame crow. "They come to take the thoughts of the princes and princesses out hunting at night. It is good for you, because now you can look at them more easily in their beds. But I hope that if you ever get a place of honor, you will show a grateful heart."

"That is not worth talking about," said the crow from the wood.

They came into the first room. The walls were covered with rose-colored silk and flowers. The dreams rushed past again, but so fast that Gerda could not see the lords and ladies. One room was more beautiful than the last. At last, they came to the bedroom.
