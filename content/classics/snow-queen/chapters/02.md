# Kai and Gerda

In a big city, there were so many houses and people that there was no room for gardens. Most people had only a few flowers in pots. In that city, two poor children lived next door to each other. They were not brother and sister, but they loved each other like a brother and a sister.

Their parents lived in two little rooms under the roofs of two tall houses. The houses stood so close together that their roofs almost touched. In each room, there was a window, and between the two windows there was a gutter. You could step across it from one house to the other.

Each family had a big wooden box in its window. In the box, they grew herbs and vegetables, and a small rose bush. The parents had an idea. They put the two boxes across the gutter, so that they reached from one window to the other. The roses and the pea plants grew long and green, and they hung down and touched each other. It looked like two walls of flowers, and between them was a little green door.

The boxes were high, and the children knew that they must not climb on them. But their parents often let them go out and sit on their small stools under the roses. There they played together, and they were very happy.

The boy's name was Kai, and the girl's name was Gerda. In winter, they could not play outside, because the windows were frozen. So they warmed a copper coin on the stove, and they put it against the ice on the window. It made a round hole, and behind the hole, there was a bright eye on each side. It was Kai on one side, and Gerda on the other.

One cold evening, the snow was falling outside. The old grandmother was telling the children a story.

"Look at the white bees," she said. "They are flying in the snow."

"Do the white bees have a queen?" asked Kai. He knew that real bees do.

"Yes, they do," said the grandmother. "She flies where the snow is thickest. She is bigger than all the others, and she never stays on the earth. She flies back up into the black clouds. On many winter nights, she flies through the streets of the town and looks in at the windows. Then the windows freeze in beautiful shapes, like flowers."

"Yes, I have seen them," said both children. They knew that it was true.

"Can the Snow Queen come in here?" asked Gerda.

"Let her come," said Kai. "I will put her on the hot stove, and she will melt."

His grandmother stroked his hair and told them another story.

That evening, when Kai was at home, half in his night clothes, he climbed on a chair by the window. He looked out through the round hole. A few snowflakes were falling. One of them, the biggest, fell on the edge of the flower box. It grew bigger and bigger. At last, it became a woman, dressed in the finest white cloth. The cloth looked like millions of small star-shaped flakes. She was beautiful, but she was made of ice, bright, shining ice. And yet she was alive. Her eyes shone like two stars, but there was no rest in them. She nodded at the window, and she waved her hand.

Kai was frightened. He jumped down from the chair. Just then, it seemed that a big bird flew past the window.

The next day, there was a clear frost. Then spring came, with the sun and the green leaves. The swallows built their nests. The windows were opened, and the children sat in their little garden again, high above the street.

That summer, the roses bloomed more beautifully than ever. Gerda had learned a song, and it was about roses. She sang it to Kai, and he sang it with her:

"Roses grow in the valley,
There we shall see the Christ child."

The children held each other by the hand. They kissed the roses, and they looked at the bright sunshine. Those were lovely summer days. How beautiful it was, among the fresh rose bushes that seemed never to stop blooming!
