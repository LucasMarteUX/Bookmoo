# The Splinter in Kai's Heart

One summer afternoon, Kai and Gerda sat together and looked at a book of pictures. There were animals and birds in it. The church clock struck five.

"Ouch!" said Kai suddenly. "Something hurt my heart! And now something is in my eye!"

Gerda put her arm around his neck. He blinked, but she could see nothing.

"I think that it is gone," he said. But it was not gone. It was one of the small pieces of the troll's mirror. It was in his eye. And a second piece had gone into his heart. Soon his heart would be like a lump of ice.

It did not hurt any more, but the piece was still there.

"Why are you crying?" he said to Gerda. "You look so ugly when you cry. There is nothing wrong with me." Then he cried, "Ugh! That rose is eaten by a worm! And that one is crooked! These roses are very ugly. They look like the boxes they grow in." He kicked the box and tore the two roses off.

"Kai! What are you doing?" cried Gerda.

He saw how frightened she was, and he tore off another rose. Then he ran into his own window and left dear little Gerda.

After that, when Gerda brought out her picture book, he said that it was only for babies. When his grandmother told stories, he always said, "But..." and when he could, he went behind her chair, and he put on her glasses and copied her voice. He did it so well that people laughed. Soon he could copy the way everyone in the street walked and talked. Everything strange and ugly in them, Kai could do. People said, "That boy has a very clever head."

But it was the glass in his eye and the glass in his heart. That was why he even teased little Gerda, who loved him with her whole soul.

His games were quite different now. They were clever games. One winter day, when the snow was falling, he came out with a big magnifying glass. He held out the tail of his blue coat, and he let the snowflakes fall on it.

"Look through the glass, Gerda," he said. Every snowflake looked much bigger. It looked like a beautiful flower, or a star with ten points. It was lovely to see.

"Look how clever it is," said Kai. "That is much more interesting than real flowers. There is not one fault in them. They are perfect, if only they did not melt."

A little later, Kai came with big gloves on his hands and his sled on his back. He shouted into Gerda's ear, "I have been allowed to go to the big square, where the other boys play!" And he was gone.

In the square, the bravest boys tied their sleds to the farmers' wagons and went for a good ride. It was great fun. In the middle of the game, a big sled came. It was painted white. In it sat a person in a thick white fur coat and a white fur cap. The sled drove twice round the square, and Kai quickly tied his own small sled to it, and he went with it. It went faster and faster, into the next street. The person who drove it turned around and nodded to Kai in a friendly way, as if they knew each other. Every time that Kai wanted to cut loose his sled, the person nodded again, and Kai stayed. They drove right out through the gate of the town.

The snow began to fall so thickly that the little boy could not see his hand in front of him. He quickly tried to let go of the rope, but it was no use. His sled was fastened to the big sled, and it flew like the wind. He cried out loudly, but nobody heard him. The snow flew, and the sled flew. Sometimes it jumped, as if it went over hedges and ditches.

He was very frightened. He wanted to say his prayer, but he could remember nothing except the multiplication table.

The snowflakes grew bigger and bigger. At last, they looked like big white hens. Suddenly, they sprang aside. The big sled stopped, and the person who drove it stood up. The fur coat and the cap were made of snow. It was a tall and slender lady, and she was dazzling white. It was the Snow Queen.

"We have driven well," she said. "But you are frozen. Come into my bearskin." She put him in her sled, next to her, and she wrapped the fur around him. He felt as if he was sinking into a snow drift.

"Are you still cold?" she asked, and she kissed him on the forehead. Ugh! It was colder than ice. It went right to his heart, which was already half a lump of ice. He felt as if he was going to die, but only for a moment. Then he felt very well, and he did not notice the cold any more.

"My sled! Do not forget my sled!" That was the first thing that he thought of. It was tied to one of the white hens, which flew behind them with it on its back. The Snow Queen kissed Kai once more. Then he forgot little Gerda, and his grandmother, and everyone at home.

"Now you will have no more kisses," she said, "or I would kiss you to death."

Kai looked at her. She was very beautiful. He could not imagine a cleverer or lovelier face. She did not look to him like ice now, as she had when she sat outside the window and waved. In his eyes, she was perfect. He was not afraid of her. He told her that he could do arithmetic in his head, even with fractions, and he knew how many square miles there were in each country. She smiled. And then it seemed to him that what he knew was very little. He looked up into the wide, empty sky, and she flew with him, high up on the black cloud. The storm sang as if it were singing old songs. They flew over forests and lakes, over seas and over lands. Beneath them, the cold wind whistled and the wolves howled. Above them flew the black, screaming crows. But higher up, the moon shone large and bright, and Kai looked at it through the long winter night. By day, he slept at the feet of the Snow Queen.
