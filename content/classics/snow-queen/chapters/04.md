# Gerda Goes to Look for Kai

But what happened to little Gerda, when Kai did not come back?

Where was he? Nobody knew. The other boys said that they had seen him tie his sled to a big sled. It went into the street and out through the town gate. Nobody knew where he had gone. Many tears were shed. Gerda cried a great deal. People said that he was dead. He had drowned in the river near the town. Oh, how long and dark those winter days were!

At last, the spring came, with warm sunshine.

"Kai is dead and gone," said little Gerda.

"I do not believe it," said the sunshine.

"He is dead and gone," she said to the swallows.

"We do not believe it," they sang. At last, little Gerda did not believe it herself.

"I will put on my new red shoes," she said one morning. "Kai has never seen them. Then I will go down to the river and ask it about him."

It was very early. She kissed her old grandmother, who was still asleep. She put on her red shoes, and she went all alone out of the town gate and to the river.

"Is it true that you have taken my little playmate?" she said. "I will give you my red shoes, if you will give him back to me."

It seemed to her that the waves nodded in a strange way. So she took off her red shoes, the dearest thing that she had, and she threw them both into the river. But they fell near the bank, and the little waves carried them straight back to her. It was as if the river would not take the dearest thing that she had, because it had not got little Kai.

But she thought that she had not thrown them far enough. So she climbed into a boat that lay among the reeds. She went to the end of the boat, and she threw the shoes again. But the boat was not tied. With her movement, it slid away from the bank. She saw it, and she tried to get back, but before she could reach the other end, the boat was more than a yard from the shore. It went faster and faster.

Little Gerda was very frightened. She began to cry. Nobody heard her except the sparrows. They could not carry her to land, but they flew along the bank and sang, as if to comfort her, "Here we are! Here we are!"

The boat went with the stream. Little Gerda sat quite still in her stockings. Her little red shoes floated behind her, but they could not reach the boat, because it was going faster.

The banks on both sides were beautiful. There were lovely flowers, old trees, and hills with sheep and cows. But there was not a person to be seen.

"Perhaps the river will carry me to little Kai," thought Gerda. This made her happier. She stood up and looked at the green banks for many hours.

At last, she came to a big cherry orchard. In it stood a little house with strange red and blue windows and a straw roof. Outside stood two wooden soldiers, who presented arms to the people who sailed past.

Gerda called to them. She thought that they were alive. But of course, they did not answer. She came quite close to them, as the stream drove the boat nearer to the land.

Gerda called still louder. Then an old, old woman came out of the house. She leaned on a big crooked stick. She wore a large sun hat, and on it there were the loveliest flowers painted.

"You poor little child," said the old woman. "How did you get on this strong, fast river, so far out in the big world?" She walked into the water, took hold of the boat with her stick, pulled it to land, and lifted Gerda out.

Gerda was glad to be on dry land again. But she was a little afraid of the strange old woman.

"Come and tell me who you are," said the old woman, "and how you came here."

Gerda told her everything. The old woman shook her head and said, "Hm! Hm!" When Gerda had told her all, and asked her if she had seen little Kai, the woman said that he had not come by. But he would certainly come. Gerda must not be sad. She must taste her cherries and look at her flowers, which were more beautiful than any picture book. Every one of them could tell a whole story.

She took Gerda by the hand and led her into the little house. She locked the door. The windows were high up, and the glass was red and blue and yellow. The daylight shone in a strange way. On the table, there was the finest fruit, and Gerda ate as much as she liked.

While she ate, the old woman combed her hair with a golden comb. Gerda's hair curled and shone like gold around her sweet little face, which was as round and as lovely as a rose.

"I have often wanted such a dear little girl," said the old woman. "You will see how well we two will live together."

And while she combed, Gerda forgot Kai more and more. For the old woman was a witch. But she was not a bad witch. She only did a little magic for her own pleasure, and she wanted to keep little Gerda. So she went out into the garden, stretched her crooked stick over all the rose bushes, and they sank down into the black earth. Nobody could see where they had stood. The old woman was afraid that if Gerda saw the roses, she would think of her own, and of little Kai, and run away.
