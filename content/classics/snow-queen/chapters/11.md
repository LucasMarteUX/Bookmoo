# Home Again

Gerda kissed Kai's cheeks, and they became rosy. She kissed his eyes, and they shone like hers. She kissed his hands and his feet, and he became strong and happy. The Snow Queen could come home now whenever she liked. His free paper was there, written in shining letters of ice.

Then they took each other by the hand, and they walked out of the great palace of ice. They talked about the grandmother, and about the roses on the roof. As they walked, the winds were quiet, and the sun came out. When they came to the bush with the red berries, the reindeer was waiting for them. He had brought another young reindeer with him. Her milk was warm, and the children drank it and kissed her on the mouth.

Then the reindeer carried Kai and Gerda to the Finland woman, where they warmed themselves in the hot room. She told them the way home. Next, they went to the Lapland woman. She had made new clothes for them, and she had prepared her sled. Both the reindeer ran by their side, and they went with them to the end of the country. There the first green leaves were coming out. Here the children said goodbye to the reindeer and to the Lapland woman. "Goodbye," they all said.

The first little birds began to sing, and the forest was full of green buds. Out of it came a beautiful horse. Gerda knew it, because it had pulled the golden coach. A young girl was riding it. She had a bright red cap on her head, and pistols in her belt. It was the little robber girl. She was tired of staying at home. She wanted to go north first, and if she did not like it, to try another part of the world. She knew Gerda at once, and Gerda knew her. It was a joyful meeting.

"You are a fine fellow, to go wandering about like this," she said to Kai. "I would like to know if you deserve that someone should run to the end of the world for you."

Gerda patted her cheek, and she asked about the prince and the princess.

"They have gone to foreign countries," said the robber girl.

"And the crow?" asked Gerda.

"The crow is dead," said the robber girl. "His tame sweetheart is a widow now. She wears a black ribbon on her leg. She cries a lot, but it is all talk. But tell me how you got him back."

Gerda and Kai told her everything.

"Snip, snap! It is all well at last," said the robber girl. She took both their hands. She promised that if she ever came through their town, she would visit them. Then she rode away into the wide world.

Kai and Gerda walked on, hand in hand. As they went, the spring became more beautiful, with green fields and flowers. Soon they knew the big town where they lived, and the high church towers, where the bells rang merrily. They found their way to their grandmother's door. They went up the stairs and into the small room, where everything was as before. The old clock said "tick, tock," and its hands pointed to the hour. But as they went through the door, they saw that they were grown up. They were a man and a woman now.

The roses on the roof were in full bloom, and they looked in through the open window. There stood the little stools where they had sat as children. Kai and Gerda sat down, each on their own stool, and they held each other's hands. The cold, empty glory of the Snow Queen's palace was gone from their minds, like a bad dream.

The grandmother sat in the bright sunshine, and she read aloud from the Bible: "Unless you become like little children, you cannot enter the kingdom of God."

Kai and Gerda looked into each other's eyes, and suddenly they understood the old song:

"Roses grow in the valley,
There we shall see the Christ child."

They sat there, grown up, but children in their hearts. And it was summer, warm, lovely summer.
