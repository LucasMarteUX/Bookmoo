# Nothing to Eat

At the edge of a great forest, there lived a poor woodcutter. He had a small house with a straw roof, a stove, and a table with four chairs. Every day, he went into the forest with his axe, and he cut wood. He carried it to the town and sold it. It was hard work, and he did not earn much. But for many years, there was enough.

The woodcutter had two children. The boy was called Hansel, and the girl was called Gretel. Their mother had died when they were very small, and the woodcutter had married again. His new wife was a hard woman. She did not like the children, and she did not hide it. She spoke to them sharply, and she gave them the smallest piece of bread.

Hansel and Gretel loved each other very much. They did everything together. Hansel was a clever boy, and he was brave. Gretel was a little younger, and she was gentle and kind. In the evenings, they sat on the step in front of the house, and they watched the sun go down behind the trees.

"One day," said Hansel, "I will be a big man, and I will build you a house of gold."

"I do not want a house of gold," said Gretel. "I only want to stay with you."

"We will always stay together," said Hansel.

Then came a bad year. The rain did not come in the summer, and the fields were brown and dry. The wheat did not grow, and the price of bread went up. Nobody in the town needed wood, because nobody had money to buy it. The woodcutter went every day into the forest, and every evening, he came home with his wood still in his cart.

Slowly, the little house became empty. There was no more meat. There was no more cheese. In the end, there was only a little bread, and then half a loaf, and the children were always hungry.

One night, the woodcutter lay in his bed and could not sleep. He turned from side to side, and he sighed. At last, he spoke in a low voice to his wife.

"What will become of us?" he said. "How can we feed the children, when we have nothing for ourselves?"

His wife was silent for a moment. Then she said, in a cold voice, "I know what we will do."

"What?"

"Early tomorrow morning, we will take the children into the forest. We will take them to the thickest part, where the trees are old and tall. We will make a fire for them, and give each of them a small piece of bread. Then we will go to our work, and we will leave them there. They will not find the way home. And then we will be free of them."

The man sat up in bed. "No!" he said. "I will not do that. How can I leave my children alone in the forest? The wild animals will come and tear them to pieces."

"You fool," said the woman. "If we keep them, all four of us will die of hunger. You may as well start to make our coffins today."

"I cannot do it," said the man. "I love them."

But she did not stop. She talked and talked. She did not let him sleep. She said the same thing again and again, until he was so tired that he did not have the strength to say no. At last, he gave way.

"All right," he said, in a broken voice. "But I feel very sorry for the poor children."

In the little room next door, the children were awake. They were too hungry to sleep, and through the thin wall, they heard every word.

Gretel began to cry. She put her face in the pillow, and her shoulders shook.

"It is the end," she whispered. "Now we are lost."

"Be quiet, Gretel," said Hansel. He sat up and took her hand. "Do not cry. Do not be afraid. I will think of something. I will find a way."

"How?" said Gretel. "We are only children."

"I do not know yet," said Hansel. "But I will."

He lay still for a long time. He listened to the sound of his father and stepmother, who had stopped talking and were breathing slowly. The house was quiet. Outside, the moon came out from behind a cloud. Its light came into the room through the window, white and clear.

When Hansel was sure that the old people were asleep, he slipped out of bed. He put on his little coat. He opened the door very, very slowly, so that it did not make a sound. And he went out into the night.
