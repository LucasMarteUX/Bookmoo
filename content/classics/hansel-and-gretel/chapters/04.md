# Lost in the Forest

They walked all night. They walked in the dark, between the black trees, and they did not know where they were going. The branches hit their faces. The roots made them trip. Several times, Gretel fell, and Hansel lifted her up. When the morning came, they were still in the forest. The trees were as thick as before.

They walked all that day, too. They walked from the early morning to the evening. The sun went across the sky and down. They were very hungry. They had only a few berries from the bushes, and they drank water from a small stream. Their shoes were torn, and their feet were sore. Gretel's dress was in pieces. Hansel's face was dirty and pale. But he kept holding his sister's hand.

"Do not stop," he said. "We will find a path soon. We must."

But they did not find a path. They found only more trees. They went deeper into the forest, farther and farther from their home, and they did not know it.

When it was dark again, they were so tired that their legs would not carry them. They lay down under a big tree and slept on the cold ground. They held each other for warmth. In the night, they heard strange sounds. An owl called. Something moved in the leaves, and Gretel hid her face against Hansel's shoulder.

"Are there wolves?" she whispered.

"I do not know," said Hansel. "But I am here."

It was the third morning since they had left their father's house. They woke up very weak. Their stomachs hurt with hunger. Gretel was crying without a sound. Hansel looked at her thin face, and he felt that he was going to cry too. But he did not.

"Let us walk again," he said. "Just a little more."

They stood up. They walked on, slowly now, and always farther into the forest. Hansel knew that if no help came, they would soon die of hunger and tiredness. He did not say it. But he saw that his sister knew it too.

At noon, they came to a place where the trees were a little thinner. The sun was bright, and the air was warm. And on a branch above them, they saw a bird.

It was a small bird, as white as snow. It sat in the sun and looked down at them. Then it began to sing. Its song was so sweet and so strange that the children stopped and listened. They forgot their hunger and their tiredness for a moment. It was the most beautiful song that they had ever heard.

When the song was finished, the bird opened its wings. It flew a short way through the trees, and it sat on another branch, and looked back at them.

"It wants us to follow it," said Gretel.

"Yes," said Hansel. "I think so."

They followed. The bird flew a little way, and it waited. When they came near, it flew again. It led them through the forest, along a narrow path that they had not seen before. The trees opened. The light became brighter. They walked for a long time, and the bird sang again from time to time, as if to say, "Come on, come on."

At last, the bird flew up to the roof of a small house, and it sat there and sang. The children stopped at the edge of the trees.

They could not believe their eyes.

The house stood in a green clearing. It was small and round and pretty. The walls were made of bread. The roof was made of cake, and it was covered with white sugar. The windows were made of clear, shining sugar, like glass. A fence of gingerbread went around the garden. There were sweets and candies on all the bushes.

"It is a house of bread!" said Gretel. She put her hand to her mouth.

"And a roof of cake," said Hansel. "Gretel, we are saved! We will eat as much as we want."

He ran forward. He reached up as high as he could, and he broke a piece off the roof. It was soft and sweet, and it smelled like honey. He put it in his mouth, and his eyes closed.

"It is wonderful!" he said. "Try it, Gretel. You can eat some of the window. It will taste very sweet."

Gretel stood on her toes, and she bit a small piece from the window pane. It was hard sugar, and it melted on her tongue. She laughed. She had not laughed for three days. She ate and ate.

Then, from inside the house, a soft voice sang:

"Nibble, nibble, gnaw,
Who is nibbling at my little house?"

The children did not stop. They only looked at each other, and they answered, with their mouths full:

"The wind, the wind,
The wind from heaven."

And they went on eating.

Hansel liked the taste of the roof so much that he pulled off a very big piece. Gretel pushed out a whole round window and sat down on the grass with it. They ate with both hands. They did not think about the voice. They did not think about anything. They only felt the sweet food and the warm sun.

And suddenly, the door of the little house opened.
