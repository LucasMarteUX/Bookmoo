# The Bread Crumbs

Not long after that, the hunger came back. The summer was hot again, and the rain did not fall. The woodcutter earned nothing. In the kitchen, there was only half a loaf of bread, and it was dry and hard.

One night, the children heard their stepmother's voice through the wall.

"Everything is eaten," she said. "We have only half a loaf left. That is all. After that, there is nothing. The children must go. This time, we will take them farther into the forest. We will take them to a place so deep that they cannot find the way back. There is no other way to save ourselves."

The father said nothing for a long time. He felt a heavy weight on his heart. "It would be better," he said at last, "to share the last piece with our children."

"You say that," said the stepmother. "But you also said it the first time. You agreed once, and you must agree again. Who says A must say B. We cannot stop now."

The woodcutter was very weak. He was tired and sad, and he was afraid of her. He had not the strength to argue. He said nothing, and the woman took this to mean yes.

Hansel was awake. He had heard everything. When the old people were asleep, he got up and went to the door. He wanted to go out and pick up more pebbles, as he had done before. But the door was locked. The stepmother had put the key on a string around her neck.

Hansel stood in the dark and held the handle. Then he went back to bed.

"What is it?" whispered Gretel.

"The door is locked," said Hansel. "I cannot go out."

Gretel began to cry again. Hansel put his arm around her.

"Do not cry," he said. "Go to sleep. The good God will help us. I am sure of it."

But he lay awake for a long time. He looked at the ceiling and thought. He could not make a plan. He only knew that he would not lose hope.

Very early in the morning, the stepmother came to the room. She pulled the children from their beds. "Get up!" she said. "We are going to the forest." She gave each of them a piece of bread. It was smaller than the time before.

They set off. Gretel held her bread under her apron. Hansel put his in his pocket. As he walked, he took a little of it, and he broke it into very small pieces, and he put them quietly on the ground. When he walked a few steps, he stopped, and put a few more.

"Hansel, why are you stopping again?" said the father. "Why do you keep looking back?"

"I am looking at my little pigeon, Father," said Hansel. "It is sitting on the roof, and it wants to say goodbye to me."

"You fool," said the stepmother. "That is not a pigeon. It is the morning sun on the chimney."

But Hansel went on throwing crumbs on the path behind him, one by one, all the way. He did not stop until the whole piece of bread was gone.

The stepmother led them deeper than before. The forest was thicker and darker. The trees were so tall that the sun hardly came through. The children had never been there in their lives. Gretel held on to Hansel's hand, and she did not say a word.

At last, they came to a small clearing. The father made a big fire, and the stepmother said, in the same cold voice as before:

"Sit here, children. If you are tired, you may sleep a little. We are going to cut wood. In the evening, when we have finished, we will come and fetch you."

They went away. The children sat by the fire. At noon, Gretel broke her bread in two pieces. She gave the larger one to Hansel, because his was gone. They ate it slowly. They listened for the sound of an axe, and this time they heard none.

After some time, they fell asleep, as they had before. The evening passed. The night came. Nobody came to fetch them.

When they woke up, it was dark and quiet. The fire was cold ash. Gretel shivered.

"Do not worry," said Hansel. "Wait until the moon rises. Then we will see the crumbs of bread which I put on the road. They will show us the way."

They waited. At last, the moon came up. It was thinner than before, and its light was pale. The children stood up and looked at the ground.

They looked and looked. They could not find a single crumb.

"Hansel," said Gretel. Her voice shook. "Where are they?"

"They must be here," said Hansel. He bent down and felt the ground with his hands. "They are here somewhere."

But they were not. In the forest, there lived many thousands of birds. In the day, they had flown down, and they had picked up every crumb. Not one was left.

Hansel stood in the dark. For the first time, he did not know what to say. The words stayed in his throat. At last, he took his sister's hand.

"We will find the way," he said. "I am sure of it."

But in his heart, he was not sure at all.
