# The White Duck and the Way Home

The children walked for two hours. They walked through the forest, and this time, they were not afraid. They felt strong. The sun was behind them, and the air smelled of pine and earth. Sometimes, they stopped to rest, and Hansel counted his pearls.

But after two hours, the trees ended. In front of them, there was a great stretch of water. It was wide and deep and dark, and it went from one side to the other as far as they could see.

"We cannot cross it," said Hansel. He walked up and down the bank. "I do not see a footbridge. I do not see a plank. There is no bridge."

"And there is no boat," said Gretel. She looked left and right. Then her face became bright. "But look, Hansel! A white duck is swimming there. I will ask her. Perhaps she will help us."

She stood at the edge of the water, and she called out in a clear voice:

"Little duck, little duck, can you see
Hansel and Gretel who wait for thee?
There is no bridge, there is no plank,
Take us across on your back, on your bank."

The white duck heard her. She turned in the water, and she swam to the bank. She was big and strong, and her feathers were as white as milk.

Hansel got on her back. "Come, Gretel," he said. "Sit behind me."

"No," said Gretel. "That would be too heavy for the little duck. She must carry us across one at a time."

The good duck took Hansel first. She swam slowly and carefully, and she put him down on the other side. Then she came back for Gretel and carried her across in the same way. When both children stood on the far bank, they thanked the duck, and she bowed her head, and she swam away.

They walked a little farther. After some time, the forest began to look familiar. They knew the shape of that hill. They knew the old tree with the broken top. They knew the smell of the earth. Their hearts beat faster.

"Gretel," said Hansel. "I think we are near!"

At last, they came to the top of a hill, and they saw it. Far away, in the valley, there was a small house with a straw roof. Smoke came up from its chimney.

"It is our house!" said Gretel.

They began to run. They ran down the hill, over the fields, and up to the garden. They did not stop. They opened the door and ran into the room, and they threw their arms around their father's neck.

The woodcutter was sitting at the table with his head in his hands. When he heard the noise, he looked up. He could not believe his eyes. For a moment, he did not move. Then he jumped to his feet and caught them both in his arms.

"My children!" he said. "My dear children!"

He cried like a child. He kissed their faces and their hands. He had not had one happy hour since the day when he left them in the forest. Every night, he had lain awake and thought about them. He had asked himself a hundred times how he could have done such a thing.

"Forgive me," he said. "Forgive me, please."

"We forgive you, Father," said Hansel. "We are home now."

The stepmother was gone. She had died while the children were away. Nobody ever told them how.

Gretel opened her apron, and she let the gold and the jewels run out onto the floor. They rolled all over the room, shining in the light. Hansel emptied his pockets next to them, handful after handful. Pearls and rings and bright stones lay on the boards in a great pile.

The woodcutter looked at it, and he did not say anything. He could not.

"It is for us," said Hansel. "It is for all three of us."

"We will never be hungry again," said Gretel.

From that day, their troubles were over. The woodcutter sold a few of the jewels, and he bought food and clothes and wood. He repaired the roof of the house. He bought a cow, and some hens, and a horse for the cart. The three of them lived together in peace. Every evening, the father sat with his children at the table. They ate bread and butter and soup, and they told stories, and they were happy.

Hansel and Gretel never forgot their time in the forest. But they did not think about it with fear. They remembered that they had stayed together. They remembered the pebbles, and the white bird, and the white duck. And they knew that they could do anything, if they did it side by side.

And that is the end of my story. Look, there runs a little mouse. Whoever catches it may make a big fur cap out of it.
