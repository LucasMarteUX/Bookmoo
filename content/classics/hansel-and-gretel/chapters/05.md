# The Old Woman and the Little House

A woman came out. She was very old, as old as the hills. She was bent and thin, and she walked with two crutches. Her face was all lines, her nose was long and sharp, and her eyes were red and small. She came out slowly, one step at a time.

Hansel and Gretel were so afraid that they let fall what they had in their hands. The piece of cake fell on the grass. The sugar window fell and broke into pieces. They stood still. They could not move.

But the old woman nodded her head. She smiled kindly. "Oh, you dear children," she said. Her voice was soft and friendly. "Who has brought you here? Come in, come in. Do not be afraid. Stay with me, and no harm will come to you."

She took them by the hand, one with each of her thin hands. She led them into the little house.

Inside, it was warm and bright. A good smell filled the room. On a table, there was a clean white cloth. And the old woman put good food in front of them: milk and pancakes with sugar, apples, and nuts. The children ate and ate. They had never had such a meal in their lives. They drank the milk, and they thanked her many times.

"You must be very tired," said the old woman. "Come. I will show you your beds."

She took them to a small room. There were two pretty little beds, with white sheets and soft pillows. The children climbed in. The sheets were clean and cool. Hansel and Gretel felt as if they were in heaven. They closed their eyes at once, and they were fast asleep.

But the old woman was not kind. She had only pretended. She was a wicked witch. She lived in the forest, and she lay in wait for children. That was why she had built the little house of bread and cake. It was a trap. When a child came to eat, she took it, and she killed it. She cooked it and ate it. Such a day was a feast day for her.

Witches have red eyes, and they cannot see far. But they have a very good sense of smell, like animals, and they can feel when a person comes near. When Hansel and Gretel came into the forest, the witch knew it. She had laughed in her little house, and she said to herself, "I have them now. They will not get away."

Early in the morning, before the children were awake, she got up. She went to the small room, and she looked down at them. They were sleeping, side by side, with rosy cheeks. They looked so plump and healthy. The witch put out her tongue and licked her lips.

"What a good meal," she said. "What a tasty little mouthful."

She took Hansel in her thin, strong hand. He was too sleepy to cry out. She carried him out of the house and across the garden, to a small stable, and she locked him in behind a door of iron bars. He woke up when the door shut. He shouted and shook the bars with all his strength. But it was no use. Nobody could hear him.

Then the witch went back to the house, and she shook Gretel until she woke up.

"Get up, you lazy girl!" she cried. Her voice was not soft anymore. It was cold and sharp. "Get up! Bring some water and cook something good for your brother. He is in the stable outside. I want him to be fat. When he is fat enough, I will eat him."

Gretel began to cry. She cried until her whole face was wet. But it was no use. She had no choice. She did what the wicked witch told her.

From that day, every day, the best food was cooked for Hansel. He had the best meat and the sweetest bread. He had butter and honey and milk. But Gretel got nothing to eat but the shells of crabs. She was thin and hungry. And every morning, the old woman went to the stable. She put her face near the bars, and she cried:

"Hansel, put out your finger. I want to feel if you are getting fat."

Hansel was a clever boy. Under the straw of the stable, he had found a small bone, the bone of a chicken. He held it out through the bars, and the old woman touched it with her thin fingers.

"What?" she said. "Still so thin! He eats so much, and he does not get fat. How can this be?"

Her eyes were red and weak. She could not see that it was only a bone. She thought that it was his finger. Every morning, she touched the bone, and every morning, she was surprised. Hansel held his breath, and he did not laugh.

A week went by. Then two weeks. Then three. Every day, the same thing happened. The old woman pushed her hand through the bars, and she touched the bone, and she cursed. She could not understand it.

After four weeks, she was tired of waiting. She stood in front of the stable with her stick, and her face was red with anger.

"Enough," she said. "I do not care any more if he is fat or thin. Tomorrow I will kill him and cook him."

She went back to the house. She called for Gretel, who was sitting in a corner. "Gretel! Come here! Fetch some water! Fill the big pot. Quick!"

Gretel stood up slowly. She went to the well, and she looked into the dark water. Her tears fell into it, one by one, and made rings on the surface.

"Dear God," she whispered. "Please help us. If the wild animals of the forest had eaten us, at least we would have died together."

"Be quiet," said the witch from the doorway. "Crying will not help you at all."
