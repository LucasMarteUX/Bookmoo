# The White Pebbles

The night was bright. The moon was almost full, and it hung over the trees like a silver coin. In front of the house, on the ground, there were many small white stones. They shone in the moonlight like pieces of real silver.

Hansel knelt down. He began to pick up the pebbles, one by one. He chose the white and shining ones. He put them in his coat pockets, first on the right and then on the left. When both pockets were full, he picked up a few more and held them in his hand. Then he went back into the house, quietly, as he had gone out.

Gretel was waiting for him in bed with wide eyes. "What did you do?" she whispered.

"Do not ask," said Hansel. "Go to sleep. Do not worry, little sister. God will not forget us."

He lay down, and he put the pebbles under his pillow. Gretel looked at him for a moment. Then she closed her eyes, and in spite of her fear, she slept.

Before the sun was up, the stepmother came into their room. She pulled off the blankets with a rough hand.

"Get up, you lazy children!" she said. "We are going to the forest to cut wood."

Hansel and Gretel got up and dressed. The woman gave each of them a small piece of dry bread. "That is for your dinner," she said. "Do not eat it before noon. You will get nothing else."

Gretel put her bread under her apron, because Hansel's pockets were full of stones. Then the four of them went out together. The air was cold, and the sky was only a little pink. They walked along the road that led to the trees.

After a few minutes, Hansel stopped. He looked back at the house.

"Hansel," said the father, "why are you looking back? Why do you stay behind? Walk fast. Do not forget to use your legs."

"I am looking at my little white cat, Father," said Hansel. "She is sitting on the roof, and she wants to say goodbye to me."

"You fool," said the stepmother. "That is not your cat. That is the sun on the chimney."

But Hansel had not been looking at a cat. He had been taking one small white pebble from his pocket and letting it fall on the road behind him. He did this again and again, every time that he stopped. Nobody saw. Nobody thought of it.

They walked far into the forest. The trees became thicker, and the light became green. At last, the father stopped in a small open place.

"Now, children," he said, "gather some branches, and I will make a fire. Then you will not be cold."

Hansel and Gretel collected sticks and dry leaves. They made a pile as high as a small hill. The father lit it, and the flames went up bright and warm.

"Now lie down near the fire," said the stepmother, "and rest. We are going to cut wood in another part of the forest. When we are finished, we will come back for you."

The children sat near the fire. At noon, they ate their bread, each a small piece. They heard the sound of an axe, far away in the trees. Knock, knock, knock. They thought that their father was working near them. They felt better.

But it was not an axe. It was a dry branch that the father had tied to a dead tree. When the wind blew, it moved to and fro and hit the trunk. That was the sound that they heard. The father and the stepmother had gone far away. They did not come back.

The fire burned low. The children sat and waited. After some time, their eyes closed. They were so tired and so full of fear that they slept deeply on the dry leaves.

When Gretel woke up, it was dark.

"Hansel!" she said. She shook him. "Wake up! It is night. We are alone. Where are Father and Mother?"

Hansel sat up and looked around. The fire was only a red spot. The forest was black, and a strange wind was in the trees. Gretel began to cry.

"How can we get out of the forest now?" she said.

"Wait a little," said Hansel. His voice was calm. "Wait until the moon rises. Then we will see."

They waited. The clouds opened, and the full moon came up, round and bright. Its light fell between the trees on the dead leaves.

Hansel took his sister by the hand. "Look," he said. He pointed at the ground.

There, in the light of the moon, the pebbles were shining. They lay along the path like a silver road, one after another, as far as they could see.

"Follow them," said Hansel. "They will take us home."

They walked all night. Hansel went first, and Gretel held his hand. They did not talk. They only followed the shining stones through the trees. Their feet were tired, and they were hungry, but they did not stop. As the sky began to turn grey, they saw the little house with its straw roof, and the smoke that went up from the chimney.

They went to the door and knocked.

The stepmother opened it. When she saw them, her face became white and then red.

"You naughty children!" she said. "Why did you sleep so long in the forest? We thought that you did not want to come back!"

But the father looked at them, and his eyes were full of tears. He opened his arms, and he held them close. He was very glad. It had cut him to the heart to leave them. He kissed them on the forehead, and he did not say anything.

For some days, there was peace in the house. Hansel and Gretel ate a little bread. They thought that the worst was over.

But it was not.
