# The Hot Oven

Early the next morning, before the sun was fully up, the witch called Gretel from her bed. The kitchen was cold and dark. A big black pot stood on the table.

"Get up," said the old woman. "Hang the pot over the fire, and fill it with water. We will bake bread first, and then we will cook your brother. I have already heated the oven, and I have made the dough."

Gretel did as she was told. Her hands were shaking. She filled the pot from the well, and she hung it on its iron hook. She put wood on the fire. Then the witch took her by the arm and pushed her toward the big stone oven in the wall.

The oven was open. From inside, red flames were jumping, and the heat burned her face.

"Climb in," said the old woman. "See if it is hot enough. Then we can put in the bread."

Gretel looked at the open mouth of the oven, and she understood. The witch did not want to bake bread. She wanted to shut the iron door, and let Gretel bake in the oven. After that, she would eat her, too.

Gretel's heart was beating very hard. She did not scream. She did not run. She stood very still, and she thought as fast as she could. She remembered Hansel and his bone. She knew that she must be as clever as her brother.

"I do not know how to do it," she said, in a small and foolish voice. "How can I get in? It looks so small."

"Silly goose," said the witch. She waved her stick. "The door is big enough. Look, I can get in myself. It is easy."

She went to the oven. She bent down and put her head and shoulders inside to show the girl. The flames lit up her grey hair.

That was the moment.

Gretel took one step back, and then she ran forward with all her strength. She gave the old woman a push. It was a very big push. The witch fell forward into the oven, and her feet went up in the air. Before she could turn around, Gretel slammed the iron door shut. She pushed the heavy bolt across, and she held it with both hands.

The witch began to scream. It was a terrible, high sound, and it went on and on. She beat on the iron door. She howled and cursed. But the oven was very strong. The door did not open. Gretel stood with her back against it and her eyes tight shut, and she did not move. After a while, the screaming stopped.

The kitchen was quiet. Only the fire made a soft noise.

The godless witch was dead.

Gretel opened her eyes. She was shaking all over, and she could hardly stand. But she did not stay. She ran out of the kitchen and across the garden as fast as the wind. She ran to the stable, and she threw open the door.

"Hansel!" she cried. "Hansel, we are free! The old witch is dead!"

Hansel jumped up. He sprang out of the stable like a bird from an open cage. He caught his sister in his arms and lifted her off the ground. They laughed and cried and kissed each other. They danced around and around in the sun. They could not stop.

"How did you do it?" said Hansel at last.

"I pushed her into the oven," said Gretel. "Like she wanted to push me."

"You are the bravest girl in the world," said Hansel.

"No," said Gretel. "I only did what you taught me. I was clever."

They sat down on the grass and took a long breath. The air was fresh, and the sun was warm. The white sugar roof of the little house shone above them.

"Now we do not have to be afraid any more," said Hansel.

"No," said Gretel. "Now we can go home."

But first, Hansel wanted to look inside the house. There were so many rooms, and the witch had lived there for so many years. The children went in together. They walked through the kitchen, past the oven, and they did not look at it.

In every corner of the house, there were chests and boxes. They were old and heavy. Hansel opened the first. It was full of pearls, hundreds of them, white and round and shining. He opened the second, and it was full of gold coins. In the third, there were rings and necklaces and bright stones in red and green and blue. The witch had taken them from all the poor travelers who had come into her wood.

"Gretel, look at this!" said Hansel. He put his hand deep into the pearls. "These are much better than pebbles!"

He filled his pockets with as many pearls and jewels as he could carry. His coat was heavy, and it clinked when he moved.

"I will take something home, too," said Gretel. She held out her apron, and she filled it with gold and stones until it was full, and she held the corners tightly.

"Now we must go," said Hansel. "We must leave this witch's forest before it gets dark."

They looked once more at the little house. The bread walls and the sugar windows did not look pretty any more. They only looked sad. Then the children turned their backs on it, and they walked into the trees.
