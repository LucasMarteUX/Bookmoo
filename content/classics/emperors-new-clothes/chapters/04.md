# The Emperor Sees the Cloth

At last, the emperor wanted to see the cloth himself, while it was still on the loom. He took a group of the most important men of the court with him. Among them were the old minister and the loud official, who had been there before. They walked together to the weavers' room.

The two swindlers worked harder than ever. Their hands moved faster and faster, and they did not use a single thread.

"Is it not magnificent?" said the old minister and the official. They had been there before, and they thought that the others could see the cloth. "Your Majesty must look at the colors. Your Majesty must admire the pattern!" And they pointed at the empty looms.

"What is this?" thought the emperor. His heart went cold. "I cannot see anything at all! This is terrible. Am I stupid? Am I not fit to be emperor? That would be the worst thing in the world."

"Oh, it is beautiful," he said aloud, and he turned to the weavers with a smile. "It has our highest approval." He nodded, and he looked at the empty loom with a happy face, because he did not want to say that he saw nothing.

The men of his court looked and looked. They saw nothing more than he did. But they all said the same thing: "It is beautiful! It is wonderful!" They advised the emperor to wear a suit made of the new cloth at the great procession, which was going to take place in a few days.

"It is magnificent," they said. "It is excellent. It is extraordinary." Everyone seemed to be delighted. The emperor gave each of the swindlers a medal to wear on his coat, and he gave them a new title. He called them "Imperial Court Weavers."

The night before the procession, the swindlers did not sleep. They stayed up all night. They lit more than sixteen candles, so that everyone could see how busy they were. They pretended to take the cloth off the loom. They cut the air with big scissors. They sewed with needles that had no thread. At last, in the morning, they said, "Now the emperor's new suit is ready."

The emperor came to the hall with all the important men of the court. The swindlers held up their arms as if they were holding something very, very light.

"These are the trousers," said the first one.

"This is the coat," said the second.

"And here is the cloak," said the first. "They are as light as a spider's web. You will feel as if you had nothing on your body at all. But that is the beauty of them."

"Oh, yes," said all the men of the court. But they could see nothing, because there was nothing to see.

"Would Your Majesty please take off your clothes?" said the swindlers. "We will help you to put on the new ones, in front of the big mirror."

The emperor took off all his clothes, and the swindlers pretended to put the new suit on him, piece by piece. They tied the cloak around his neck. They adjusted the trousers. They smoothed the coat on his shoulders. The emperor turned around and around in front of the big mirror.

"How well it looks!" said everyone. "How well it fits! What a pattern! What colors! It is a magnificent suit!"

The man in charge of the ceremony came in. "Your Majesty, the men with the canopy are waiting outside," he said. "They are ready for the procession."

"I am ready, too," said the emperor. He looked in the mirror again, and he turned a little to the left and a little to the right. He wanted the people to think that he admired his own beautiful clothes. "Does it not fit me wonderfully?"

The servants who were going to carry the long train of his cloak bent down. They put their hands on the floor, as if they were picking up something heavy. They walked forward, and they held up the air. They did not want anyone to know that they could not see anything.
