# The Two Weavers

"What a wonderful cloth," thought the emperor. "If I wear a suit made of this cloth, I will be able to see which men in my empire are not fit for their jobs. I will know the clever men from the stupid ones. I must have this cloth made for me at once!"

He called for the two weavers. They came into the great hall, and they bowed low. They had fine coats and long black hair, and they looked very serious.

"We hear that you make a special cloth," said the emperor.

"It is true, Your Majesty," said the first swindler. "It is the finest cloth in the world."

"It is also the most special," said the second. "Only wise people can see it."

"I want a suit made of it," said the emperor. "I will pay you well. How much money do you need?"

The swindlers looked at each other. Then the first one named a very, very big number. The emperor did not care. He gave them a bag of gold at once, so that they could begin without losing a minute.

The weavers were shown to a big room in the palace. They asked for two looms, which are big machines for making cloth. They put them in the middle of the room, and they began to work. They moved their hands up and down, and left and right. They pulled the strings. They pushed the pedals with their feet. They looked very busy.

But there was no cloth on the looms. There was nothing at all.

"We need the best silk," they said to the servants. "And we need gold thread. Only the best can make a suit for the emperor."

The servants brought the finest silk and the most precious gold thread. The swindlers took it all, and they put it quickly into their bags. They worked at the empty looms until late at night, and they did not put in a single thread.

A few days went by. The emperor was very curious. "I wonder how the weavers are getting on," he thought. "I would like to see the cloth."

But then he felt a little worried. He remembered the secret of the cloth. A person who was stupid, or who was not fit for his job, could not see it. He was not afraid for himself. Of course, he was sure that he was both clever and fit. But it might be wiser to send someone else first.

"It is better that I do not go myself," he said. "I will send somebody to look, and then he will tell me."

All the people in the city knew about the power of the cloth. Everyone wanted to know who in the town was stupid, or who was not good at his job. They talked about it in the shops, in the street, and at the well. Every person hoped that his neighbor would not see the cloth. Of course, nobody thought about himself.

"I will send my old minister," said the emperor. "He is an honest man. He is very intelligent. Nobody understands his work better than he does. He is the best person to see the cloth."
