# The Honest Old Minister

The good old minister walked down the long hall of the palace. He was a thin man with white hair and a kind face. He wore round glasses, and he carried a small book under his arm. He had served the emperor for many years. He knew everything about the work of the empire.

He opened the door of the weavers' room, and he went in. The two swindlers were sitting in front of the two looms, with their hands in the air.

"Good morning, honorable minister," said the first swindler. "Please come nearer."

The minister walked forward. He looked at the loom. He opened his eyes wide.

"Heaven help me!" he thought. "I cannot see anything at all!"

The loom was empty. There was nothing on it. There was no cloth, no thread, no color. But he did not say a word.

"Do you not admire the pattern?" said the second swindler. He pointed to the empty loom with a proud smile. "Do you not love the colors?"

The old minister looked and looked. He tried his best. He pushed his glasses up his nose. He leaned forward. But no matter how hard he looked, there was nothing to see.

"Oh dear," he thought. "Can it be that I am stupid? I have never thought so. I have always been proud of my mind. But nobody must know. Is it possible that I am not fit for my office? No, no, I cannot tell them that I do not see the cloth."

"Well?" said the first swindler. He pretended to be busy with his weaving. "Have you nothing to say?"

"Oh, it is very pretty," said the old minister. His voice shook a little. He looked at the loom through his glasses. "It is very beautiful indeed. What a lovely pattern! What bright colors! I will tell the emperor that I like the cloth very much."

"We are so glad to hear it," said the two weavers. They described the colors and the pattern in great detail. "This is a rich blue, like the sea at night. And this part is a deep gold, like sunlight in the autumn. Do you see this curving line? It is the most difficult part of the pattern."

"Yes, yes," said the minister. "I see it." He listened closely, so that he could tell the emperor everything. When he went back to the palace, he told the emperor all that the weavers had said.

The swindlers then asked for more money, and more silk, and more gold thread. They said that they needed it to finish the work. The emperor sent it at once. They kept everything for themselves. Not one thread went on the loom. They went on weaving at the empty looms, as they had before.

After a few days, the emperor sent another man to see the cloth. This was an honest official of the court, a tall man with a loud voice. He looked at the empty looms, just as the minister had done. He looked and looked, but he could see nothing, because there was nothing to see.

"Is it not a beautiful piece of cloth?" asked the two swindlers. They pointed to the pattern that did not exist.

"I am not stupid," thought the man. "So it must be that I am not fit for my important position. That is very strange. But I must not let anyone know." He praised the cloth, which he could not see. He said how much he loved the colors and the fine pattern.

"It is excellent, Your Majesty," he told the emperor. "It is the finest thing that I have ever seen."

Soon everyone in the city was talking about the wonderful cloth.
