# The Tower and the Spindle

On the morning of her fifteenth birthday, Briar Rose woke up early. The sun was shining through her window. Birds were singing in the garden. She put on her pink dress, and she ran down the stairs.

But the castle was strange and empty. In the great hall, there was nobody. In the kitchen, there was only the cook, and she was busy.

"Where are my mother and father?" asked Briar Rose.

"They have gone out, Your Highness," said the cook. She looked away. "The king had some important business in the next town. The queen went with him. They will be back this evening."

"On my birthday?" said Briar Rose. She was surprised and a little sad.

"They did not want to go," said the cook. "But they could not say no. They told me to give you a kiss for them."

Briar Rose sighed. She took a piece of bread, and she went out into the garden. She walked among the roses and talked to the birds. But the day was long, and she was alone. She did not know what to do.

"I will explore the castle," she thought. "I have lived here all my life, but there are many rooms that I have never seen."

So she went in again, and she began to walk through the great house. She opened doors and looked into rooms. There were big rooms with high ceilings and long tables. There were small rooms with dusty books and old chairs. She found a room full of old clothes, and she tried on a hat with a feather. She found a room full of broken toys. She went up stairs and down stairs. She walked through long, quiet halls, where her steps made an echo.

At last, she came to an old tower in a corner of the castle. She had never been there before. The door was low and dark, and a narrow stone staircase went up in a turning line. She began to climb it.

The stairs went up and up. They were cold and old, and they were covered with dust. At the top, there was a small door. It had a rusty lock, and in the lock there was a golden key. Briar Rose stopped. She looked at the key. It was shining, as if somebody had used it that morning.

"Who lives here?" she said softly.

She turned the key. The lock gave a small click, and the door sprang open.

Inside, there was a tiny room with a little window. The sunlight came in through the glass, and it made a yellow square on the floor. In the light, an old woman was sitting on a chair. She was bending over a wheel, and her hands were busy. She was spinning thread, and the wheel made a soft, steady sound: buzz, buzz, buzz.

The old woman looked up. She did not seem to be surprised. She nodded and smiled, and she went on with her work.

"Good day, old mother," said Briar Rose. "What are you doing there?"

"I am spinning, my child," said the old woman, and she nodded her head again. She hummed a little tune while the wheel turned.

Briar Rose had never seen a spinning wheel. The king had burned all of them, and in the whole castle, nobody had ever told her what they were. She came nearer, and she looked at it with wide eyes. The wheel went round and round, and the thread came out of the old woman's hands, long and thin and shining.

"What is that thing that turns so quickly?" she asked. "It is so pretty."

"This is a spinning wheel," said the old woman. "And this is a spindle. It makes the thread."

"May I try?" said Briar Rose. "Please? I have never done it."

The old woman looked at her for a moment. Then she held out the spindle.

Briar Rose took it. It was a smooth, thin stick with a sharp point at the end. She held it in her fingers and tried to turn it as the old woman did. She felt it spin. It was cool, and it was lighter than she thought.

But she was not used to it. Her hand moved too fast, and the point of the spindle touched her finger.

"Ouch!" she said, and she dropped it. A small drop of blood appeared on her finger. It was very red, and it looked very bright in the sun.

She looked at it in surprise. "It is only a little cut," she said.

Then she felt a strange tiredness go through her body. Her head became heavy. Her eyes began to close. The room began to turn, slowly, like the wheel. She put out her hand to hold on to something, but there was nothing there. She tried to say a word, but her lips would not move.

"I am so sleepy," she whispered.

She fell down on the floor, next to the old woman's feet, and her eyes closed. A smile was on her face. She looked as if she were dreaming a good dream.

The room was quiet. The old woman looked down at the girl, and she did not move. She did not say anything. Slowly, she lifted her hand, and she touched the girl's forehead. Then her own head dropped to her chest, and her hands fell still in her lap.

The spinning wheel stopped turning. The yellow light stayed on the floor of the little room, and no one moved. And in the whole tower, in the whole castle, a deep and heavy quiet began.
