# The Thirteenth Wise Woman

On the day of the feast, the castle shone like a jewel. Flags flew on every tower, and the doors stood open. Musicians played in the garden. The kitchens were full of good smells: roast meat, hot bread, and cakes with honey. The guests came from every part of the country. They came on horses and in carriages, and their clothes were red and gold and blue.

In the great hall, the long tables stood in rows, and the king and queen sat at the head of the room. Next to them, in a small golden cradle, lay the baby princess. She was dressed in white, and she was fast asleep.

The twelve wise women came last. They walked into the hall together, and the people stopped talking. Each of them wore a tall red cap and red shoes. Each of them had a long white stick in her hand. They did not look old, and they did not look young. Their eyes were clear and deep, and their steps made no sound.

The king stood up and bowed. "Welcome," he said. "You are our most honored guests. Please sit and eat."

They sat at the table, and they ate from the twelve golden plates. They drank wine from silver cups. They spoke softly about the weather and the harvest, and they smiled at the king and the queen. When the feast was over, they stood up, and they formed a circle around the cradle.

"Now," said the oldest of them, "it is time for our gifts."

The first wise woman stepped forward. She touched the baby's forehead with her white stick. "I give you goodness," she said. "You will always have a kind heart."

The second came forward. "I give you beauty," she said. "No one in the world will be more lovely than you."

The third said, "I give you riches. You will never be poor."

The fourth said, "I give you a gentle voice. When you speak, people will want to listen."

The fifth gave her a happy heart, and the sixth gave her great wisdom. The seventh gave her skill with her hands, and the eighth gave her a beautiful singing voice. The ninth gave her the gift of dancing, and the tenth gave her the love of animals. The eleventh gave her good health.

The queen listened, and tears of joy ran down her face. The king held her hand. "What wonderful gifts," he whispered. "She will have all that is good in the world."

The eleventh wise woman stepped back. Now there was only one more.

But at that moment, there was a loud noise outside. It was the sound of horses and of shouting. The big doors of the hall flew open with a crash. A cold wind came in, and the candles shook.

A woman stood in the doorway.

She was tall and thin. She wore a black cap and black shoes, and she carried a broom in her hand. Her face was pale and sharp, and her eyes were full of anger. It was the thirteenth wise woman.

"Who is this?" said the king. He stood up. His face went white.

"I am the one you forgot," said the woman. Her voice was cold and hard, and it went through the room like ice. "I live in this country, just like the others. I have as many years as they have. I have as much power. But you did not invite me. Why? Because you had no plate for me? I did not need a golden plate. I only wanted to be asked."

"Please," said the queen. "We did not want to forget you. It was a mistake."

"A mistake," said the old woman. "I know about your mistakes. And I have a gift for your daughter, too."

She walked slowly across the hall. Nobody moved. She stopped in front of the cradle, and she looked down at the sleeping child. Then she raised her stick.

"The king's daughter," she said, in a loud, terrible voice, "will have a long and happy life, until she is fifteen years old. On her fifteenth birthday, she will prick her finger on a spindle. And then she will fall down dead."

The hall was silent. The queen cried out. She put her arms around the cradle. The king stood still, and he could not speak.

The thirteenth wise woman turned around. She did not say another word. She walked out of the hall, and the doors closed behind her with a bang.

For a long time, nobody moved. Then the guests began to whisper. The queen cried and held her child. The king put his face in his hands.

"What can we do?" he said. "Oh, what can we do?"

Then a soft voice spoke. "Wait."

It was the twelfth wise woman. She had not yet given her gift. She had stayed behind the others, and she had listened to every word. Now she came forward and stood in front of the king and the queen.

"I cannot take back the wish of my sister," she said. "A wish like this is too strong. It must come true. But I have not yet given my gift, and I can make the wish smaller."

"Please do," said the queen. "Please."

The twelfth wise woman touched the baby's head. "The princess will not die," she said. "When the spindle hurts her, she will fall into a deep sleep. And she will sleep for one hundred years."

A great sigh went through the hall. The queen kissed the wise woman's hand. "Thank you," she whispered. "Thank you."

But the king knew that it was still a terrible thing. A hundred years! His daughter would be young, while all the people she loved would grow old and die. He looked at the cradle, and then he looked at the door where the black-capped woman had gone.

"I will not let it happen," he said quietly. "I will find a way."
