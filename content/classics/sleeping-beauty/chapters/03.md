# The King Burns the Spindles

After the feast, the king did not sleep for many nights. He walked in the halls of the castle with a candle in his hand. He thought about the black-capped woman, and about her cruel words. He thought about the spindle.

A spindle is a thin, pointed stick. In those days, people used it to make thread from wool. Every house in the country had one. Every grandmother knew how to use it, and every girl learned to spin when she was small. Spinning wheels stood in every kitchen and in every farm, and no one ever thought that a spindle could be dangerous.

Now the king thought of nothing else.

"If there is no spindle in the country," he said to himself, "then the princess cannot prick her finger. And if she cannot prick her finger, then the wish cannot come true."

In the morning, he called all his servants and his soldiers to the great hall. He stood on the steps in front of them, and his voice was clear and strong.

"Listen well to my order," he said. "From today, there will be no spindle in my kingdom. Not one. You will go to every town and every village. You will go to every farm and every house. You will take every spindle and bring it here. And then we will burn them all."

The soldiers looked at each other. They did not understand. "Even the old women's spindles, Your Majesty?" asked one.

"All of them," said the king. "Every one. And whoever hides a spindle will be punished."

The soldiers bowed, and they rode out of the castle gates. For many weeks, they went from house to house. At first, people were surprised and angry. They did not want to give up their spindles. But when they heard the story of the princess and the wise woman, they understood. Slowly, they began to bring their spindles to the soldiers. Some of them cried. An old woman on a farm kissed hers and said goodbye.

At last, in the big courtyard of the castle, there was a great pile of spindles. There were big ones and small ones. There were old ones, black with age, and new ones that were still white. The king stood in front of them with a burning torch in his hand. He looked at the pile for a long time. Then he put the torch to the wood.

The fire was hot and bright, and the smoke rose high into the sky. It burned all day and all night. The people of the town stood and watched it from far away. When the morning came, there was only a heap of grey ash.

"It is done," said the king. He felt a little easier in his heart. "Now my daughter is safe."

"Do you think so?" said the queen quietly.

"I do," said the king. "I must."

The years went by. The little princess grew, and she was a joy to everyone. She learned to walk, and then to run. She learned to speak, and then to sing. And every gift that the wise women had given her came true, one after another.

She was so good that she never said an unkind word. She was so beautiful that people stopped in the road when she passed. She was so wise that old teachers asked her questions. She sang as sweet as a bird, and she danced like the wind in the grass. The animals of the garden loved her. Birds came and sat on her hands. A small deer followed her down the path, and the cats of the castle slept on her bed.

The people of the country loved her, too. They called her Briar Rose, because in the summer she loved to walk near the wild rose bushes by the garden wall, and she had a pink dress the color of the flowers. Soon everybody used the name. Even the king and the queen called her Briar Rose.

On her birthdays, the whole castle was happy. Every year, there was a small party, and the cook made a big cake. But every year, as the day came closer, the king and the queen became quiet. They looked at the calendar, and they counted the years.

When Briar Rose was ten, the queen said to the king, "Five more years."

When Briar Rose was twelve, the king said, "Three more years."

When Briar Rose was fourteen, neither of them said anything. They only looked at each other.

She was a young woman now. She was tall and graceful. Her hair was long and golden, and her eyes were as blue as the summer sky. Her laugh was like the sound of water. She did not know about the wise women's wish, because her parents had never told her. They did not want to make her afraid. They wanted her to be happy for as long as she could.

On the evening before her fifteenth birthday, the queen sat on the edge of her daughter's bed. She stroked her hair for a long time.

"What is it, Mother?" asked Briar Rose. "Why are you so quiet?"

"Nothing, my dear," said the queen. "I only like to look at you. Tomorrow you will be fifteen. You are almost a woman."

"I know," said Briar Rose with a smile. "I want to have a big party."

"We will," said the queen. She kissed her daughter's forehead. "We will have the biggest party in the world."

But in her heart, she was afraid. She did not sleep that night.
