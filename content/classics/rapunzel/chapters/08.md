# The Empty Nest

The prince came to the tower as the first stars were out. He stood at the foot of the wall, and he looked up at the little window. His heart was light. In his coat, he had a new skein of silk. Tonight, he thought, the ladder will be finished.

He called, in the voice that he knew so well:

"Rapunzel, Rapunzel,
Let down your hair to me."

The window opened. The golden hair came down. He took hold of it and began to climb.

It seemed a little different tonight. It was a little stiffer, and it felt colder in his hands. But he did not think about it. He climbed up with a smile on his face. He thought of her eyes and her voice. He pulled himself up over the sill of the window.

"Rapunzel," he said, "I am here—"

He stopped.

In the room, there was only one lamp. It burned low, and the light was yellow and weak. There was a person in the chair. It was not Rapunzel. It was a tall old woman, in black, with grey hair and a long thin face. She was looking at him with eyes full of hate.

"Aha," said the witch. "So you have come to fetch your dearest."

The prince stood still. He looked around the room. The bed was empty. The books were on the shelf. There was no one else in the tower.

"Where is she?" he said. "Where is Rapunzel?"

"Your beautiful bird is no longer in her nest," said the witch. She stood up, and she laughed in a mocking voice. "She does not sing anymore. The cat has taken her. And the cat will scratch out your eyes also, before it is done."

"What have you done to her?" said the prince. His voice was hoarse.

"Rapunzel is lost to you," said the witch. "You will never see her again. You will not hear her sing. You will never hold her hand. She is gone, and she will not come back."

The prince cried out. His face went as white as paper. He felt a sharp pain in his chest, as if a knife had gone through it. He did not know what to do. He did not know what to think. He only knew that the woman he loved was lost, and that it was his fault.

"No," he said. "No!"

He stumbled to the window. He looked down at the ground, so far below, dark under the trees. In his pain and his despair, he did not think. He put one foot on the sill, and he jumped.

He fell for a long time, it seemed. The wind rushed past him. The ground came up fast. Then he struck the thorn bushes at the foot of the tower. They broke his fall, and he did not die. But the long sharp thorns went into his face and into his eyes.

He lay on the ground and screamed. He put his hands to his face. Everything was dark. He could not see the stars. He could not see his own hands. He tried to open his eyes, but he saw nothing, nothing at all.

"I am blind," he whispered. "I am blind."

For a long time, he lay where he fell. Above him, in the window, the witch looked down. She did not feel sorry. She closed the window.

When he was able to stand, the prince began to walk. He did not know where he was going. He walked with his hands in front of him, and he fell, and he stood up again. He lost his horse, and he lost his way. He went deeper into the forest, away from the tower, away from the people who knew him. He did not want anyone to see him. He did not want anyone to pity him.

He ate roots and berries. He drank from streams. At night, he slept on the cold ground, and in the morning, he walked again. He did not think about his father or his castle. He thought only about Rapunzel. He called her name sometimes, in a low voice, to the empty wood, and the trees did not answer.

"Where are you?" he said. "Are you alive? Are you safe?"

He cried so often that his face was always wet. His clothes became torn and dirty. His hair grew long. He became thin and weak, and nobody who saw him would have known that he was the son of a king.

Days went by. Then weeks. Then months. The seasons changed, and the prince did not see them change. He only felt the heat of the summer and the cold of the winter. He walked on, and he did not know that he was walking toward the place he was looking for.

Years passed. Many years. He wandered through the forest, and then through the hills, and then through the wide, dry land. His heart was broken, but he did not stop. Somewhere inside him, there was a small light. It was a hope that he did not want to put out. He did not know where it came from.

And one day, in the late afternoon, he stopped. He lifted his head. He heard something on the wind.

It was a voice.
