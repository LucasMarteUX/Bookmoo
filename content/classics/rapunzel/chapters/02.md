# The Man in the Witch's Garden

The man waited for the evening. All day, he thought about his plan. He walked around the wall twice, and he looked for a place where he could climb. Near the back of the garden, he found a spot where the stones were old and uneven. There was a place for his hands and for his feet.

When the sun went down, the town became quiet. The man stood in the dark in front of the wall. His heart was beating hard. He was not a thief, and he had never stolen anything in his life. But he thought of his wife, who lay in bed with her pale face, and he began to climb.

The stones were cold and rough. He went up slowly, with his fingers in the cracks. At the top, he stopped and looked down. The garden was dark and beautiful. The flowers smelled sweet. In the light of the stars, he could see the pale shapes of the apple trees. Nothing moved.

He let himself down on the other side. He walked quietly along the path. He found the bed of green leaves, and he knelt and quickly picked a big handful. Then he ran back to the wall, climbed up and over, and ran home without looking back.

His wife was waiting. When she saw the green leaves, she cried out with joy. She did not even wash them. She made a salad at once, and she ate it all, and she ate it so fast that she did not speak. It was the best thing that she had ever tasted. Her cheeks became pink again, and her eyes became bright.

"Thank you," she said. "Oh, thank you!"

The man smiled. He was happy and relieved. He thought that the problem was over.

But the next morning, the woman woke up with the same hunger. It was not smaller. It was three times stronger. She looked at her husband with pleading eyes.

"I need more," she said. "I must have more. I will not have any peace until I eat it again."

"But, my dear—"

"Please," she said. "Please."

The man sighed. He knew that there was no way to say no. He waited for the night, and he went to the wall again. This time, he did not feel so afraid. He thought, "I have done it once, and nothing happened. I can do it twice."

He climbed the wall. He let himself down into the garden, and he walked quickly across the grass. But when he came to the bed of rapunzel, he stopped. His whole body went cold.

Someone was standing there in the dark.

It was a woman. She was old and tall, and she was dressed in black. Her hair was grey, and her face was long and hard. In the light of the moon, her eyes seemed to shine like the eyes of a cat. She stood with her arms crossed, and she looked at him without moving.

"So," she said. Her voice was very quiet. "It is you. I wondered who was stealing my rapunzel."

The man could not speak. His knees were shaking. He knew who she was. Everybody knew. This was the witch, and she was looking at him with a terrible anger.

"How dare you," she said. "How dare you come into my garden like a thief and take my plants! You will pay for this."

"Please," said the man. He fell on his knees in front of her. "Please, have mercy on me. I did not want to do it. I did it because I had to."

"Had to?" said the witch. "Nobody has to steal."

"My wife is going to have a baby," said the man. The words came out quickly. "She saw your plants from our window. She looked at them, and she wanted them so much that she became ill. She said that she would die if she could not eat some. I could not let her die. So I came. I am very sorry. I will do anything you ask."

The witch looked at him for a long time. The wind moved in the leaves of the apple trees. At last, the hard look in her face became a little softer, and she gave a slow nod.

"If it is as you say," she said, "then I will not punish you. You may take as much rapunzel as you want. You may take it all. But I have one condition."

"Anything," said the man.

"When your wife has her baby, you will give it to me."

The man felt as if the ground was falling away under him. "My child?" he whispered.

"Your child," said the witch. "I will take it as my own. I will care for it as if I were its mother. It will have everything that it needs. It will not be hungry, and it will not be cold. You have my word."

"But..." said the man.

"That is my price," said the witch. "Or you may go to the town judge, and tell him that you stole from my garden. You choose."

The man looked at her. He was so afraid that he could not think. He thought of his wife in bed, and of the town judge, and of the long cold night. In his terror, he did not see any other way. His mouth opened, and he heard his own voice say, "Yes."

"Good," said the witch, and she smiled a thin smile. "Pick your rapunzel."

He picked as much as he could carry. His hands were shaking. He went back over the wall, and he did not tell his wife anything. He gave her the leaves, and she ate them gladly. Her health came back. She looked well and strong, and her husband tried to smile.

But at night, when she slept, he lay awake and looked at the dark ceiling. He did not know how he could tell her.
