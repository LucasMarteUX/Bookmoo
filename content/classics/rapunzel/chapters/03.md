# The Baby Named Rapunzel

The months went by. The man did not tell his wife about the witch. He told himself that it was not a real promise. A promise made in fear, he thought, is not a promise at all. Perhaps the witch would forget. Perhaps she was only trying to frighten him. He worked in the day, and he tried not to think about it at night.

In the winter, the snow fell on the roofs and on the wall of the garden. In the spring, the snow melted. And one warm morning, when the first birds were singing, the baby was born.

It was a girl. She was small and pink, with a round face and a little tuft of fine, golden hair. She opened her eyes, and she looked at her mother. The woman cried with joy. She held her close, and she put her face against the soft head.

"Look at her," she whispered. "Look at our daughter."

The man stood at the bed, and he looked at them. His heart was full of love. But it was also full of fear.

In the evening of that same day, there was a knock at the door.

The man opened it. The witch stood on the step. She was dressed in black, as before, and her grey hair was tied behind her head. She did not smile. She did not say hello. She walked into the house as if it were her own.

"I have come for the child," she said.

"No," whispered the woman in the bed. She looked at her husband. "What does she mean? What child?"

The man could not look at her. He told her everything in a low voice. He told her about the garden, and the second night, and the bargain. When he finished, there were tears in his eyes.

"You promised her our baby?" said the woman. "You promised her our daughter?"

"I was afraid," said the man. "I did not think it was real. Please forgive me. I did not know what else to do."

The woman held the baby tighter. She looked at the witch. "Please," she said. "Please, do not take her. We will give you anything else. We will give you everything we have."

"I do not want your things," said the witch. "I want what I was promised. A promise is a promise." She held out her thin hands.

"Please," said the woman again, and she began to cry.

The witch's face did not change. "I will take good care of her," she said. "She will not be hungry, and she will not be cold. You will not find a better home for her anywhere. But she is mine now."

The man put his hand on his wife's shoulder. "We must," he said in a broken voice. "We gave our word. If we do not, I do not know what she will do to us. Or to the child."

For a long moment, the room was silent. The woman looked at the baby's small face. Then, with trembling arms, she gave her to the witch.

"What is her name?" asked the witch.

"We have not chosen one yet," said the woman, in tears.

The witch looked down at the baby. "Then I will name her," she said. "Her name will be Rapunzel. For the plant that bought her."

She wrapped the child in her black cloak, and she walked out of the door. The man and the woman stood in the dark room and heard her steps fade away into the night. They did not see their daughter again for many years.

The witch took the baby to a house deep in the forest. She raised her there. And Rapunzel grew up, and she grew up well. She had food and warm clothes. She had a soft bed with a blue blanket. The witch was not kind in her words, but she did not hurt the child. She taught her to read, and to sing, and to sew. She taught her the names of the stars. She was strict, but she was not cruel.

Rapunzel loved to sing. She sang when she woke up in the morning, and she sang when she went to sleep at night. She sang to the birds in the trees. And as she grew, her hair grew too. It was a wonderful, long, golden hair, finer than silk, and brighter than the sun on wheat. By the time she was ten, it was so long that it touched the floor. The witch brushed it every evening, and she did not let anyone cut it.

"You have the most beautiful hair in the world," she said. "Never cut it. It is your treasure."

Rapunzel did not understand why. But she was a good child, and she obeyed.

She was lonely, though, even then. The witch was often away. Sometimes, she was gone for a whole day. Rapunzel played alone in the garden of the house in the forest. She talked to the squirrels, and she made flowers into crowns. She never saw other children. She did not know that there was a town beyond the trees, or that somewhere a man and a woman remembered her name, though they had never said it.

When she was twelve years old, the witch came to her with a serious face.

"Rapunzel," she said. "It is time. I have a new home for you."
