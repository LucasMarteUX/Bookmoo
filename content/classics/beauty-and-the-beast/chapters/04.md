# The Beast's Price

The Beast was the most frightening thing that the merchant had ever seen. He was very tall, and he was covered with thick dark hair. He had large hands with long nails, and a heavy head with sharp teeth. His eyes were fierce and yellow. He wore a rich coat of velvet, but it did not make him look gentler. It made him look more strange.

"Who gave you permission to take my roses?" he said. His voice was deep and rough, like thunder. "I gave you shelter from the storm. I gave you food and a warm bed. I did not ask anything in return. And this is how you thank me? By stealing from my garden? You are an ungrateful man! You will pay for this!"

The merchant dropped the rose. His knees gave way, and he fell on the ground. "Please, my lord," he said, "forgive me. I do not want to offend you. I am very grateful for your kindness. It was so generous that I did not think that you would mind a flower. I only wanted it for my daughter. She asked me for a rose, and I had nothing else to give her."

"Do not call me 'my lord,'" said the Beast. "I am not a lord. I am a Beast. And I do not like flattery. You say that you have a daughter? Then I will tell you how this can be settled. You have done wrong, and you must die. But I will give you a chance. You can ask your daughter to come to me. If she is willing to take your place, I will let you go."

"What?" said the merchant. His face turned white.

"One of your daughters must come here of her own free will," said the Beast. "She must come in three months. If none of them will come, then you must come yourself, and I will kill you. I will give you three months to say goodbye. That is all."

The merchant could not speak. He thought, "I will not send my child to this monster. I will come back myself, in three months. At least I can see my family once more." So he said, with a shaking voice, "I promise."

"Good," said the Beast. "I will not send you home with empty hands. There is a large chest in the room where you slept. Fill it with anything you want. Take it with you. Now go."

The merchant went to the room as if he were walking in a dream. He found a big chest in the corner, and he opened it. It was empty. He began to fill it with gold coins from a heap that lay near the wall. The more he put in, the more there was. At last, the chest was full, and he closed it and had to drag it to the door. A servant that he could not see lifted it onto the horse's back.

The merchant mounted his horse, and he rode out of the castle gate. A moment later, the gate closed behind him with a bang. The warm air was gone. The snow was falling again, and the wind was cold. The castle disappeared among the trees. He rode on for a whole day, and in the evening, he came to his cottage in the forest.

The children ran out of the door. They were so happy to see him. "Father! Father is home!" they cried. They took him in their arms and helped him down from his horse. Then they saw his white face, and his tears, and the great chest on the horse.

"What has happened?" said the oldest son. "Why are you crying?"

"Come inside," said the merchant. "I must tell you everything."

By the fire, with all his children around him, the merchant told them about the storm and the castle and the rose. He told them about the Beast and his promise. He took out the rose, and he held it in his hand. Then he gave it to Beauty.

"Here, my child," he said. "Here is your rose. It will cost me my life. I cannot tell you how sorry I am."

Beauty took the rose. She looked at it. Her eyes were wet, but she did not cry. She was thinking.

The sisters were not so silent. "It is all because of you!" said the oldest, and she pointed at Beauty. "If you had asked for a dress, like us, none of this would have happened. You wanted to be different. You are very proud of yourself. Now our father will die because of your foolish rose!"

"Do not cry for him, Beauty," said the second sister, with a bitter laugh. "You must be so happy to make him suffer."

"Why should I cry?" said Beauty quietly. "My father will not die. The Beast said that he would take one of us instead. I will go to him."

"No!" cried the merchant. "I will not allow it!"

"Father," said Beauty, "listen to me. I did not ask for a dress or a jewel. I asked for a flower. It is my fault that you were in that garden. I do not want you to suffer for what I wanted. If I do not go, you will die. I would rather be eaten by the Beast than lose my father. I am not afraid."

"My dear child," said the merchant. "You do not know what you say."

"I know exactly what I say," said Beauty. "I am young, and you are not. You have worked for all of us. You must live. I will go."

Her three brothers stood up. "We will go to the castle and kill the Beast," said the oldest. "We will fight him. We are strong."

"You cannot," said the merchant. "I saw him. He is a monster. He has the strength of ten men. And the castle is full of magic. No, I will not let you try."

For a long time, the family argued. The father said no, and the sons said no, but Beauty did not change her mind. In the end, she said, "I will go, with you or without you. I have decided." And they knew that she meant it.

The two sisters were very glad. They did not say it aloud, but in their hearts, they were happy to see her go. They were jealous of her goodness, and they were tired of living with her.
