# "Will You Marry Me?"

The Beast stopped in the doorway. He was very big, and he almost filled it. His face was fierce, but his voice, when he spoke, was low and almost gentle.

"Good evening, Beauty," he said.

"Good evening, Beast," she said. She tried to sound calm, but her voice shook.

He came into the room. He did not come near her. He stopped on the other side of the table, and he looked at her with his yellow eyes. "Did you come here of your own free will?" he asked.

"Yes," said Beauty.

"Then you are a good girl, and I am very much obliged to you," said the Beast. "Honest man," he added, in a lower voice, as if he spoke to himself. "He has a good daughter." Then he said, "Tomorrow, your father will be safe. He may keep his chest of gold. I will never ask him for anything again. And you may stay here as long as you wish. If you are not happy, you will tell me. Do you understand? I do not want to make you afraid."

"I understand," said Beauty. She looked at his large hands, resting on the back of a chair. They were hairy, but they were very still, and they did not hold anything dangerous. "Thank you for your kindness."

"It is nothing," said the Beast. "Everything in this castle is yours. If there is anything you want, you only have to ask. Whatever you wish will be done. Now I must ask you one question. Tell me honestly. Do you not think that I am very ugly?"

Beauty did not answer for a moment. She did not like to lie. She said, "That is true. I cannot tell a lie. I think that you are very ugly. But I believe that you are very good."

"You are right," said the Beast sadly. "I am ugly. And I have no wit. I know very well that I am only a poor stupid beast."

"A person who thinks he is stupid is not very stupid," said Beauty. "A fool does not know it."

"Then eat, Beauty," said the Beast. "And try to enjoy yourself in your new home. Everything here is yours. I should be very sorry if you were not happy."

"You are very kind," said Beauty. "I am a little less afraid. I must tell you the truth, though. It is only your heart that I am beginning to like. When I think of your face, I am still frightened."

"Ah," said the Beast. "I know. My heart is good, but I am still a monster."

They sat for a time in silence. Then, very simply, he got up. "Good night, Beauty," he said.

"Good night, Beast."

He went out. His steps were heavy in the hall, and soon they were gone.

That night, Beauty lay in her great bed with white curtains. She did not sleep well. She heard the clocks of the castle strike the hours. She thought about her father, and about the Beast, and about the strange house. At last, she fell asleep, and she had a dream. A tall lady in a white dress stood next to her bed. Her face was kind, and her hair was silver.

"Beauty," said the lady, "you gave up your happy home to save your father. You will not be sorry for your good heart. Do not judge by what you see. Look at the heart."

Beauty wanted to ask a question, but the lady was gone. In the morning, the sun came in, and she found that she was not afraid.

The days passed. Soon she had a regular life. She got up late, and she took a long breakfast in the garden. She read books in the library. She played the harpsichord and sang. She walked in the garden for hours, among the roses and the fountains. She talked to the birds, and she found that some of them came to her hand. She was never bored. Every day, she found something new: a hidden path, a picture, a song.

There was no one in the castle with her. But invisible hands served her meals, and invisible voices said, "Good morning, Beauty." Once, she heard laughter in a hall, and she followed it, but the hall was empty. It was a strange life, but it was a good life.

Every evening at nine o'clock, the Beast came to supper. At first, they sat together without much to say. But little by little, he began to talk. He spoke slowly, with long pauses. He told her about the garden, and about the stars, and about the weather. He asked about her family. He listened with great attention to everything that she said. Beauty found that his talk was not stupid at all. He was simple and honest, and he said what he thought. She stopped being afraid of his face, and she began to wait for his visit.

Every evening before he left, the Beast asked her the same question. He stood at the door, and in a low voice, he said:

"Beauty, will you marry me?"

And every evening, Beauty answered gently, "No, Beast."

He did not argue. He did not seem angry. He only bowed his head, and sighed, and said, "Good night, Beauty." Then he went away. She felt sorry for him, and she did not know why.

"I cannot do it," she thought. "I like him very much. I think that he is a good friend. But I do not love him. I cannot marry someone I do not love. I would not be telling the truth."

The weeks passed. The spring turned to summer, and the summer turned to autumn in the garden, although the leaves did not fall. Beauty had been in the castle for three months. She was happy. Only one thing made her sad. She missed her father.

One evening, when the Beast asked his question, and she gave her answer, he did not leave at once. He stood with his hand on the door, and he said, "You are not unhappy, Beauty? I hope that you do not think of going away."

"I am happy here," said Beauty. "But I am worried about my father. I have not seen him for three months. I do not know how he is."

She looked in the mirror in her room that night. It was the same mirror that had shown her the cottage on the first day. She said, "I wish to see my father." The glass shook and grew bright. And she saw the room in the cottage. Her father lay on a bed, thin and pale. A doctor stood at his side. The sons were around the bed with sad faces. Her father's lips moved. She could hear what he said. He said, "Beauty. Where is Beauty?"

She turned away from the mirror. There were tears on her face.
