# The Castle in the Snow

The light was far away, but it was clear and warm. It shone between the black trunks like a yellow star. The merchant took his horse by the rein, and he walked toward it. The snow was deep, and the wind pushed him, but he did not stop.

As he came nearer, he saw that the light came from the window of a great castle. He had never seen it before, although he had lived near the forest for years. It stood on a hill at the end of a long avenue of trees. And a strange thing happened. When he turned into the avenue, the snow stopped. There was no wind. The air became soft and warm. The trees on both sides were orange trees, and they were full of white flowers and golden fruit.

"I must be dreaming," said the merchant.

He walked up the avenue. At the end, there was a wide stone court, and in front of it, a flight of steps made of shining stone. Torches burned on the walls. The big doors were open.

"Hello!" he called. "Is anyone here?"

Nobody answered.

He went in. He walked through a great hall with a high ceiling. He walked through a room with a long table, and through a room full of paintings, and a room full of gold lamps. Every room was bright, and every room was empty. In the end, he came to a smaller room. A good fire was burning in the fireplace. In front of it, a comfortable couch stood, with soft cushions.

"Someone is expecting a guest," said the merchant. "But it is not me. Perhaps the owner will come soon. I will sit down for a moment."

He sat down by the fire. He was cold, and wet, and very, very tired. He took off his wet coat and hung it near the flames. The warmth made his eyes heavy. In a few minutes, he was asleep.

When he woke up, it was late. The fire was still burning, but the room was quiet. He felt very hungry. He had eaten nothing for a whole day. And then he saw that a small table stood next to his chair, and on it there was a good dinner. There was hot soup and roast chicken, fresh bread, a bowl of fruit, and a glass of red wine.

He looked around. "Who put this here?" he said. "Is anyone there?"

There was no answer. The room was empty.

The merchant was too hungry to wait. "I will thank the owner when I see him," he said, and he ate. The food was delicious, the best that he had tasted for years. When he finished, he felt strong again. He sat for a while by the fire, and he waited for somebody to come. Nobody came. At last, his eyes closed again, and he slept.

He slept through the whole night. In the morning, when he woke up, the sun was shining through the window. And on the little table, there was a new meal. It was a warm cup of chocolate, with cakes and fruit.

"This is very strange," said the merchant. "Who is looking after me? Who is my kind host?"

He walked through all the rooms again. He looked in every corner. He opened every door. There was no servant, no cook, and no master. There was only silence. Sometimes he thought that he heard a soft footstep, or a door closing, but when he turned around, nobody was there.

At last, he went out into the garden. It was a wonderful garden. Although it was winter everywhere else, here there was spring. The sun was warm, the birds were singing, and the flowers were in bloom. There were fountains and statues, and beds of colored flowers, and long walks under the trees. He thought that he had never been so happy to look at anything.

"All this is so beautiful," he said. "It seems that it was prepared for me. I will go and fetch my children. They can all come and live here. We will be happy again."

He went to the stable. His horse stood there, and it had a full bag of oats. It looked well and strong. The merchant patted its neck. He decided to ride home at once, and bring his children back to the castle.

He took the path that led to the stable. On each side of the path, there was a hedge of roses. They were red and white and pink, and they were the most beautiful roses that he had ever seen. Their smell filled the air like perfume.

The merchant stopped. He suddenly remembered Beauty's request.

"A rose," he said softly. "She asked me only for a rose. And I have no money to buy anything else for her. Surely the owner of this castle will not mind a single flower, after all the hospitality that I have received."

He looked at the hedge and chose the best flower. It was a deep red rose, still wet with dew. He took it in his fingers, and he broke it from the branch.

At once, there was a terrible noise behind him. It was a deep roar, as if the earth was shaking. The merchant turned round with the rose in his hand.

Behind him stood a Beast.
