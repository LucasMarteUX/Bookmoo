# The Two Statues and the Wedding

The next morning, a great carriage drove into the courtyard of the merchant's little house. It was a carriage with four white horses, and gold on the doors. Footmen in blue and silver jumped down, and they opened the door. The merchant stood at the window of his cottage and could not believe his eyes. Out of the carriage stepped Beauty. She wore a gown of silk, and she was laughing. Next to her walked a young man in a blue velvet coat.

"Father!" she cried. She ran to him, and she kissed him on both cheeks. "Father, I want you to meet someone. This is the prince of the castle. He is the one that you called the Beast."

The merchant stared at the young man. He rubbed his eyes. He looked at the carriage and at the footmen. For a moment, he could not speak. Then the prince came forward, and he bowed low.

"Sir," he said, "I owe you an apology. I was cruel to you. I frightened you and I took your word. I was under a bad spell, but that is no excuse. Your daughter has freed me. I love her with all my heart, and I want to marry her. I hope that you will give us your blessing."

The old man looked at his daughter. She nodded and smiled. He saw how happy she was.

"My dear boy," he said. His eyes filled with tears. "I do not know what to say. I thought that I had lost her. Now I see that I have found a son. Yes. Yes, of course. You have my blessing, and all my heart."

There was a great feast in the cottage that day. The merchant's three sons came, in their soldiers' uniforms, and they kissed their sister. They shook the prince's hand. They said that they had always known that something good would happen.

The two sisters, who lived in town, heard the news that same afternoon. They ran to the cottage in their best dresses. They had come to see if it was true. They stood at the door, and they looked in.

Beauty was sitting at the table next to the prince. She was radiant, and her eyes were bright. The prince was handsome and kind, and he spoke to her with love. The merchant sat at the head of the table in a new coat, and the sons and the servants and the guests were laughing and singing. The two sisters were so jealous that they could not speak.

"This is the Beast?" said the oldest, in a low voice. "He is young! He is handsome!"

"He is a prince," said the second, and she bit her lip. "A real prince. And she is going to be a princess. It is not right."

"What do we do?" said the oldest. "We cannot be nice to her now."

But they did not have time to do anything. The fairy was standing behind them. She had come into the room without a sound. She was tall, and her dress was white, and her eyes were stern.

"Girls," she said quietly. "I know what you did."

The sisters turned around. They went white.

"You told lies to your sister," said the fairy. "You cried false tears. You made her break her promise. You did it because you envied her, and because you wished her to be punished. You are proud and hard of heart. Because of you, a good and gentle prince almost died."

"We... we did not know," said the oldest. Her voice shook.

"You knew," said the fairy. "And you did not care. You have a hard heart, and so I will give you a hard body. You will be turned to stone. You will stand at the gate of your sister's palace, and you will watch her happiness every day. You will see her kindness, and you will hear her laugh. You will not move, and you will not speak. You will stay there until you understand what you have done. When you see your faults, and when you are truly sorry for them, you will become human again. But I think that this will take a long time."

She raised her hand. There was a flash of light, and a sound like the closing of a door. The two sisters were gone. Two statues of grey stone stood on the grass in front of the cottage. They were two beautiful ladies, with their faces turned to the sky.

Nobody at the feast said a word. The merchant looked at Beauty. She had tears in her eyes.

"It is harsh," she said quietly. "I did not want that. They are my sisters."

"It is not forever," said the fairy. "They can change. It depends on them." She smiled. "You are very kind, Beauty. But it is not your fault. It is a good lesson, and it may do them good."

She went out of the house, and she disappeared into the evening light.

A week later, the wedding was celebrated in the great castle. It was the most splendid wedding that anyone had ever seen. The guests came from all the kingdoms around. The halls were full of flowers. Four hundred candles burned in the great ballroom. The people of the court danced and sang, and Beauty wore a dress of white silk with a red rose in her hair. It was the rose of the first garden, the one that her father had picked.

The prince took her hand. "Do you remember the first night?" he asked. "When I asked you to marry me?"

"I remember," said Beauty. "I said no."

"And I said good night," said the prince. "I always hoped."

"I am glad that you did," said Beauty.

They lived together in the castle for the rest of their lives. The merchant lived with them, in the best room, and he was never poor again. The prince and Beauty ruled the land with kindness and wisdom. Their house was always full of friends, and of roses. Every year, on the day of their wedding, they walked together in the garden and picked a single red rose.

And at the gate of the palace, the two grey statues stood and watched. Nobody knew how they came there, but they were there on the morning of the wedding, and they never moved. Some say that, in time, the sisters learned to be sorry. But that is another story.
