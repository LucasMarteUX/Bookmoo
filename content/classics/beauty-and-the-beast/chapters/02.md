# The Ship and the Rose

Beauty's work soon made the little house a better place. She planted vegetables in the garden. She hung a clean white curtain in the window. She sang while she cooked, and the house was no longer so quiet. In the evening, she read aloud to her father and brothers by the fire, and she played the old flute that she had saved. The men worked hard all day, but they were happy to come home to her.

Her sisters did not change. They stayed in bed until ten o'clock. They did not help in the house. When they saw Beauty working, they laughed at her.

"Look at her," said the oldest. "She likes being poor. She is so low and ordinary. She does not even know that she is a servant."

"She is not a lady," said the second. "She is a kitchen girl."

Beauty did not answer. She only smiled, and she went on with her work.

A year went by. Then it was a second year. The family became used to their life. The three sons learned to farm, and the fields gave a little wheat and many vegetables. They were poor, but they were not hungry. Only the two sisters were still sad. Every day, they talked about the parties they had missed and the dresses they could not wear.

Then, one spring morning, a messenger came to the door. He was out of breath from running. He had a letter for the merchant.

The merchant opened it. He read it twice. His face changed. Then he looked at his children, and his eyes were bright.

"My friends," he said. "I have good news. One of my ships, the one that we thought was lost, is safe. It has come into port with all its goods. It is full of silk and spices. If I can reach the city, I can sell them."

"We are rich again!" cried the oldest daughter, and she jumped up. "Oh, I knew that it would happen!"

"We will go back to the city," said the second. "We will have new dresses and new shoes! We will have parties!"

"Wait, wait," said the merchant, laughing. "I must go first. I must go to the port and see the ship. It is a long road, and the weather is bad. I do not want to take you with me until I am sure."

The sisters were not happy about this, but they agreed. Then they began to ask for presents.

"Father," said the oldest, "bring me a dress of the finest silk. And a hat with feathers."

"Bring me a gold necklace," said the second, "and a pair of red shoes."

The sons also asked for things: a horse, a book, a warm coat. The merchant wrote everything down. Only Beauty said nothing. She stood at the stove and stirred the soup.

"And you, Beauty?" said her father. "What will you have? Do not be shy."

"Nothing, Father," said Beauty. "I only wish that you come home safely. That is the only present I want."

Her sisters looked at her angrily. They thought that she wanted to make them look greedy. "She is only pretending to be good," they whispered.

But the merchant smiled. "You must ask for something, my child," he said. "You are young. Every girl likes a pretty present."

Beauty thought for a moment. "Then bring me a rose," she said. "There are no roses here. I have not seen one since we came to the forest. I love them so much."

"A rose," said the merchant. He wrote it down. "That will be easy."

The next day, he left. He rode away on his old horse, down the road between the trees, and all the children waved until he was gone.

He reached the city in a few days. But when he came to the port, he heard bad news. His old partners had thought that he was dead. They had divided the goods from the ship, and they had sold them. There was nothing left for him. He went to the court of justice, and he talked to the judges, and he pleaded for his rights. It took many months. He spent all his money on lawyers and on food. At the end of it, he was as poor as before. He had only enough money to pay for his journey home.

It was winter when he started out. The days were short, and the cold was bitter. He rode all day through a wind that cut like a knife. When he came near the forest, the snow began to fall. It was thick and heavy, and it covered the road. By evening, he could not see anything. His horse was tired, and the merchant himself was weak and hungry. He had not eaten since morning.

He had to cross the great forest. It was a day's journey, and he knew the way. But the snow made everything look strange. When the night came, he was lost. The wind blew so hard that it threw him from his horse. He lay in the snow, and he felt that he was going to die.

Then he heard a sound. It was a sound that made his blood go cold. It was the howling of wolves. The sounds came from every side, and they were coming nearer. He got up and held his horse by the rein. He did not know which way to go. He did not dare to move.

"Dear God," he said. "I have come all this way and found nothing. Please help me."

At that moment, he saw a light between the trees.
