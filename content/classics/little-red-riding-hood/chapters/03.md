# The Wolf in the Wood

Inside the wood, it was cool and green. The sun came through the leaves in long, golden lines, and the air smelled of earth and pine. Little Red Cap walked slowly, as her mother had said. She listened to her own steps on the path. She listened to the wind in the trees.

The girl was happy. She thought about her grandmother's face when she opened the door. She thought about the cake, and the warm tea they would drink together. She even began to sing a little song.

Then she heard a sound behind a big tree. It was a soft sound, like a heavy animal walking on dry leaves.

She stopped. She looked to the left. She looked to the right.

A big grey wolf stepped out onto the path.

He was very tall. His fur was thick and dark, and his ears stood up straight. His eyes were yellow, and they were very bright. He looked at the girl for a long moment. Then he smiled, and his teeth were long and white.

Now, Little Red Cap did not know that wolves are dangerous. Nobody had told her this. In the village, there were only stories about wolves, and she thought stories were just stories. She did not feel afraid. She only felt a little surprised.

"Good morning, Little Red Cap," said the wolf. His voice was soft and friendly.

The girl was surprised again. "He knows my name!" she thought. But she remembered her mother's words: be polite. So she said, "Thank you, wolf. Good morning to you."

"Where are you going so early, Little Red Cap?" asked the wolf.

"I am going to my grandmother's house," said the girl.

"I see," said the wolf. He looked at the basket. "And what do you have there under the white cloth?"

"Cake and wine," she said proudly. "Yesterday was baking day. My grandmother is ill and weak, so my mother made a cake for her. It will make her strong again."

"What a kind thing to do," said the wolf. "Your grandmother is a lucky woman. And where does she live, Little Red Cap? Is it far from here?"

The girl did not see any danger in this question. She answered it with a smile.

"Her house is in the middle of the wood," she said. "You walk for a quarter of an hour along this path. Then you see three big oak trees. Her house stands under them. And there are nut trees just below the house. You must know it."

"Oh yes," said the wolf. "I think I know that house."

But the wolf was lying. He was not thinking about the house. He was thinking about something else. He looked at the girl, and he looked at the basket, and he thought: "What a soft, young thing! She will taste much better than the old woman. But I want both of them. I must be clever, and I must be quick. Then I can eat the old woman and the girl."

So the wolf began to walk next to her. He did not leave her side. His steps were slow and soft, like her steps. To anyone who saw them, it looked like a friendly walk between two travelers.

For some minutes, nobody said anything. The path went on and on between the trees. The girl looked at the wolf from time to time. His coat was beautiful, she thought, and he did not look angry at all. Perhaps wolves in real life were not so bad. Perhaps the stories were wrong.

The wolf watched her too. He was looking for the right moment. He was thinking of a way to make her leave the path and go away from her grandmother's house. He needed time. If he could only make her stop and play, then he could run to the house first.

Suddenly, the wolf stopped. "Look, Little Red Cap," he said. "Look around you!"

The girl looked up.

On both sides of the path, there were flowers. There were red ones and yellow ones, white ones and blue ones. They stood in the long grass, between the trees, and they moved a little in the wind.

"Did you not see them?" asked the wolf. "They are so pretty, and there are so many of them. Why do you not look at them?"

The girl said nothing. She was looking at the flowers, and her eyes were very wide.
