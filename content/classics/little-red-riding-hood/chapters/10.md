# The Second Wolf

People in the village like to tell another story about Little Red Cap. They say that it happened some weeks later.

By then, the grandmother was well again. She could walk and work in her garden. But she liked to have visitors, so Little Red Cap often went to see her. Her mother baked a cake every week, and every week the girl took it to the house under the oak trees.

This time, Little Red Cap walked along the path very carefully. She did not look at the flowers. She did not listen to anyone who called to her from the trees. Her eyes were on the path in front of her, and her feet stayed on it.

She was about half way along the road when she heard a voice. It was a low, smooth voice, and it came from behind a bush.

"Good morning, little girl."

She stopped. She looked at the bush. Two yellow eyes looked back at her.

It was another wolf.

This one was not as big as the first. He was thin and grey, and his fur was old and rough. He had a clever, bad look on his face.

"Where are you going, little girl?" said the wolf. "It is such a lovely day. Come into the wood with me, and I can show you something wonderful."

But Little Red Cap remembered what had happened before. She was not afraid, but she was not silly either.

"No, thank you," she said. "I must stay on the path. I am going to my grandmother's house, and my mother is waiting for me to come home."

She did not wait for an answer. She walked on, and she walked faster. She did not run, but her steps were quick. The wolf stood where he was and watched her go. He licked his lips, and he began to follow her, quietly, through the trees.

When she got to the grandmother's house, Little Red Cap did not wait. She knocked at the door three times, and then she said her name in a low voice. The door opened, and she went in quickly. She closed the door behind her.

"What is it, child?" said the grandmother. "You are breathing very fast."

"Grandmother, there is another wolf," said the girl. "He spoke to me on the path. He said, 'Good morning, little girl.' But his eyes were very bad. If we had not been on the road, I think that he would have eaten me!"

The old woman's face became serious. "Then we will shut the door," she said. "And we will lock it. And we will not open it for anyone."

She got up. She put a heavy bar across the door, and she pulled the curtains over the little window. The room was dark, and the girl and her grandmother sat together by the fireplace. Soon they heard steps outside. Slow, soft steps.

Knock, knock, knock.

"Open the door, Grandmother," said a sweet voice. It was thin and high, but it was a wolf's voice. "It is Little Red Cap. I am bringing you cakes!"

The grandmother and the girl looked at each other. They did not speak, and they did not move. The wolf waited. Then he knocked again, louder. "Grandmother! Open the door!"

Nobody answered. The wolf walked around the house two or three times. He tried the window, and he tried the back door. All of them were closed. Then he looked up, and he saw the roof of the house. It was low, and he could reach it. With one jump, he was on the straw.

"I will wait here," he thought. "The girl must go home. When she leaves in the evening, I will follow her in the dark, and then I will catch her."

But the old woman was clever. She sat very still and listened. She heard the wolf's feet on the roof, and she knew exactly what he wanted.

"Child," she whispered. "He is on the roof. He is waiting. But I have an idea. Do you see that big stone trough in front of the house? It is the place where we give water to the animals."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"Yesterday, I made sausages. I boiled them in a big pot. The water in the pot is still on the stove. It smells very strong. Take the pail, and carry that water to the trough. Carry it slowly, and pour it in. Do it until the trough is full."

Little Red Cap did not ask why. She took the pail. She opened the door a little, and she went out and poured the water into the trough. Then she came back for more. She did this again and again. Up on the roof, the wolf watched her go, and he did not move.

At last, the trough was quite full. A warm, rich smell rose from it, and the wind carried it up to the roof. The wolf lifted his nose. He sniffed. He sniffed again. It was a wonderful smell, of meat and salt and fat. His stomach said, "Look!"

He moved nearer to the edge of the roof. He stretched out his neck and looked down. Then he stretched out his neck a little more. The smell was so strong. He could not stop himself.

Then the straw slid under his feet. He tried to hold on, but he could not. He slipped, and he fell off the roof, head first, straight into the big stone trough. The water closed over him. The trough was deep, and the wolf could not climb out. He tried, but he was not strong enough. In a short time, he was gone.

In the quiet room, the grandmother waited. Then she smiled. "I think it is finished," she said.

Little Red Cap ran to the window and looked out. The trough was full and still. The wolf was not on the roof, and there was no sound outside. She laughed out loud, and she threw her arms around her grandmother.

That evening, the girl walked home happily through the wood. The sun was setting, and the leaves were gold. Nobody stopped her. No eyes watched her from the trees. She walked on the path, and she sang her little song. When she came to her village, her mother was waiting at the gate, and she hugged her very tightly.

"I stayed on the path," said Little Red Cap. "I always stay on the path now."

And after that, nobody ever tried to hurt her again.
