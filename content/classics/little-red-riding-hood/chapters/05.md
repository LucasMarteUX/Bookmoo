# Knock, Knock

The grandmother's house stood under three tall oak trees. It was a small house with a straw roof and a green door. There was a little window with white curtains, and a garden with some herbs in front. On this summer morning, the house was very quiet.

Inside, the grandmother was lying in her bed. She was old, and she was ill. For many days, her legs had been weak, and she could not stand up for long. A cup of tea stood on the small table next to her bed. A grey cat was sleeping at her feet. She was looking at the ceiling, and she was thinking about her granddaughter.

"Little Red Cap will come soon," she said to herself. "She often comes at this time. I hope she brings some of her mother's cake."

Then she heard a knock at the door.

Knock, knock, knock.

"Who is there?" she called. Her voice was thin and tired.

Outside, the wolf stood next to the green door. He was breathing fast from his long run. He cleared his throat, and he made his voice high and soft and sweet.

"It is Little Red Cap," he said. "I am bringing you cake and wine. Please open the door."

The grandmother smiled. "My dear child!" she thought. "She is early today." She tried to get up, but her legs were too weak.

"Lift the latch, my dear," she called. "Just push it up, and the door will open. I am too weak to get out of bed."

The wolf lifted the latch. The door opened with a small sound. He walked in, and he closed the door behind him.

The grandmother looked up. The room was dim, and her eyes were not good. But she saw something big and grey in the doorway. It was not a little girl.

"Who..." she began.

But she did not have time to say more. The wolf walked to the bed without a word. He did not say hello. He did not say anything at all. In a moment, it was over. The poor old woman was gone, and the room was quiet again.

The grey cat jumped from the bed. It ran under the table, and it did not come out again for a long time.

The wolf stood by the bed for a moment, and he licked his lips. He was pleased with himself. "The first part is done," he said. "And now for the second part."

He looked around the small room. There was a wooden chair next to the bed, and on the chair there were the grandmother's clothes. He saw a long nightgown, and a big white nightcap with a ribbon.

"Yes," he said. "This will do very well."

He put on the nightgown. It was too small for him, and it looked very funny, but he did not care. He pulled the nightcap over his head, far down over his ears, so that nobody could see them. Then he climbed into the bed. He pulled the blanket up to his chin, and he turned his face a little to the wall.

At last, he reached out one long paw and pulled the curtains of the bed closed.

Now the room looked like it always did. There was an old woman in a bed with the curtains closed. She seemed to be resting. If someone came in quickly, they might not look closely. They might think that everything was fine.

The wolf lay still and waited. His yellow eyes were open in the dark, and he listened to the sounds outside. He heard the wind in the oak trees. He heard the birds. He heard nothing else.

"She will come soon," he thought. "She picks flowers, that silly girl. She is slow. But she will come."

He was right.
